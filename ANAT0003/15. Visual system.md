# 2. Retina (Input Stage)

## 2.1 Image formation
- Lens focuses image onto retina
- Vision is **binocular** → both retinas receive almost entire visual scene
- Small monocular peripheral regions exist

## 2.2 Retinal structure (deep → superficial)
1. **Photoreceptors**
    - Rods
    - Cones
2. **Horizontal cells**
3. **Bipolar cells**
4. **Amacrine cells**
5. **Ganglion cells** (output neurons)
![[Pasted image 20260412003506.png|275]]

## 2.3 Rods vs Cones

### Rods
- High sensitivity (low light)
- Saturate easily
- Single wavelength → **no colour (greyscale vision)**

### Cones
- Lower sensitivity (daylight vision)
- **3 types (different wavelengths)** → colour vision
	- S → blue
	- M → green
	- L → red

## 2.4 Fovea (high acuity region)
![[Pasted image 20260412005925.png]]
- Located on visual axis
- **High cone density**
- Responsible for:
    - High spatial resolution
    - Colour vision

- Periphery: much more sensitive in low light condition


## 2.5 Retinal processing → ganglion outputs

Retina performs **pre-processing (not just passive sensing)**
### Ganglion cell types:

- **M-type (Magnocellular)**
    - Light vs dark (luminance)
- **P-type (Parvocellular)**
    - Red vs green (colour)
    - High acuity
- **Non-M non-P**
	- Many different types
    - e.g. blue vs yellow
### Key principle:
- **Receptive field size↓ →  Acuity↑**
    - Small RF → high acuity (fovea, P-type)
    - Large RF → low acuity (periphery, M-type)


## 2.6 Centre-surround receptive fields in GC

### Structure:
- **Centre**: excitation OR inhibition
- **Surround**: opposite effect
### Types:
- ON-centre / OFF-surround
- OFF-centre / ON-surround
### Function:
- Detect **contrast & edges**
- Enhance spatial differences

# 4. Primary Visual Cortex (V1 / BA17) = Striate Cortex

## 4.1 Location & definition

| ![[Pasted image 20260412012733.png\|300]] | ![[Pasted image 20260412014005.png\|150]]<br>![[Pasted image 20260412014109.png\|175]] |
| ----------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

- Located along **calcarine fissure**
- Corresponds to **BA17**
- Dense LGN input forms a white stripe (line of Gennari) that is visible to naked eye, splitting L4 into 2 layers - **'Striate Cortex'**
- Characterised by an **expanded L4** (split into sub-layers)
### Input segregation:
- M → Layer IVCα
- P → Layer IVCβ
- K → superficial layers

## 4.3 Retinotopic mapping
![[Pasted image 20260412015011.png|300]]
### Key rules:
- Each hemisphere = **contralateral visual field**
- Visual field is **inverted** along:
    - Vertical axis: upper field → lower cortex
    - Horizontal axis: left field → right cortex
    - Central-periphery axis: central field → peripheral/posterior cortex
		- Fovea → occipital pole (posterior end)
		- Periphery → anterior end of V1
### Clinical relevance: visual lesionses
![[Pasted image 20260412044156.png|325]]
- Different lesion locations → predictable visual field defects

## 4.3 Functional cell types

### Simple cells
- Respond to:
    - Specific **orientation**
    - Specific **location** in VF
- Centre-surround structure: in adjacent VF, correctly oriented stimulus will supress firing
### Complex cells
- Respond to orientation
- But in many different locations in VF

## 4.4 Columnar organisation
### 3 main patterns:
1. **Ocular dominance columns**
    - Each ODC prefers input from a particular eye
	    - Cossponds to greater input from specific layers of LGN
2. **Orientation columns**
    - Same preferred orientation
3. **Blobs / interblobs**
	- Blobs: cytochrome oxidase staining; Interblobs: non-staining
    - Function not well understood. May linked to colour processing (K input)

# Example Questions

## Overview

The visual system is a hierarchical and parallel processing system that transforms light stimuli into neural representations of the external world. Its organisation spans from the retina to the cortex and is characterised by both **serial processing through the geniculostriate pathway** and **parallel processing into dorsal and ventral streams**. In addition, secondary non-geniculostriate pathways contribute to visually guided behaviours .

Visual processing begins in the **retina**, where the optical system of the eye focuses light onto photoreceptors. Vision is largely binocular, meaning that both retinas receive input from most of the visual field, with only small monocular peripheral regions . The retina is not merely a passive sensor but performs significant preprocessing. It consists of five main layers arranged from deep to superficial: photoreceptors (rods and cones), horizontal cells, bipolar cells, amacrine cells, and ganglion cells, whose axons form the optic nerve.
 
Photoreceptors are specialised for different aspects of vision. **Rods** are highly sensitive and operate under low-light conditions but saturate easily and do not encode colour. In contrast, **cones** function in daylight and are responsible for colour vision, with three types (S, M, L) tuned to different wavelengths . The **fovea**, located on the visual axis, has a high density of cones and provides high spatial resolution and colour perception, while the peripheral retina is more sensitive in low light. Retinal processing culminates in ganglion cell outputs, which exhibit **centre-surround receptive fields** (ON-centre/OFF-surround and vice versa), enabling the detection of contrast and edges.

From the retina, visual information is transmitted via the optic nerve, with **partial decussation at the optic chiasm**, ensuring that each hemisphere processes the contralateral visual field . Signals then reach the **lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN)** of the thalamus, where information is organised into six layers. These layers maintain **eye-specific segregation**, with three layers receiving input from each eye. Functionally, the LGN separates information into **magnocellular (M), parvocellular (P), and koniocellular (K) pathways**. M pathways process luminance and motion with large receptive fields, P pathways encode colour (red-green) and fine detail with small receptive fields, and K pathways contribute additional colour processing channels .

From the LGN, visual information is relayed to the **primary visual cortex (V1, BA17)** located <font color="#c00000">along the calcarine fissure</font>. V1 is characterised by a prominent <font color="#c00000">layer IV</font>, split into sublayers by the <font color="#c00000">line of Gennari</font>, reflecting <font color="#c00000">dense LGN input</font>. Importantly, LGN inputs remain segregated: <font color="#c00000">M inputs project to layer IVCα, P inputs to IVCβ, and K inputs to superficial layers </font>. V1 is organised <font color="#c00000">retinotopically</font>, meaning that spatial relationships in the visual field are preserved in cortical maps.<font color="#c00000"> Each hemisphere represents the contralateral visual field, and the representation is inverted both vertically and horizontally</font>. The fovea is mapped to the posterior occipital pole, while peripheral vision is represented more anteriorly.

Within V1, neurons are specialised for <font color="#c00000">feature detection</font>. **Simple cells** respond to <font color="#c00000">specific orientations at precise locations in the visual field</font>, while **complex cells** respond to orientation across a broader spatial range . V1 is further<font color="#c00000"> organised into columns</font>, including ocular dominance columns (eye preference) and orientation columns, reflecting functional modularity.

Beyond V1, visual processing continues in the **secondary visual cortex (extrastriate cortex; BA18 and BA19)**, including areas such as V2, V3, V4, and V5/MT. These regions extract increasingly complex features, including form, colour, motion, and depth. For example, <font color="#c00000">V2 </font>processes <font color="#c00000">edge ownership and binocular disparity</font>, <font color="#c00000">V4</font> is involved in <font color="#c00000">colour perception and shape processing</font>, and<font color="#c00000"> V5/MT</font> encodes <font color="#c00000">motion</font>, with lesions causing akinetopsia .

At higher levels, visual processing diverges into two major pathways. The **dorsal stream** projects from V1 through V2 to areas such as <font color="#c00000">V5/MT and V3A</font>, ultimately reaching the parietal cortex. It is responsible for motion perception, spatial processing, and visually guided action, often described as the “where” or “how” pathway. In contrast, the **ventral stream** projects to temporal cortex via V3 and V4 and is involved in object recognition, shape, and colour processing, forming the “what” pathway .
