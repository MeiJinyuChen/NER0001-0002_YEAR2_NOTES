## Function: “Where / Action pathway”
### A. Pathway
- Occipital → Parietal cortex
### B. Functions
- Visuospatial processing
- Action guidance
- Body-centered representation
### C. Nomenclature
![[Pasted image 20260410094922.png]]
### D. Disorders
#### 1. Unilateral spatial neglect
![[Pasted image 20260410095350.png|375]]
- Usually **right TPJ (temporo-parietal junction) / IPL (inferior parietal lobule) damage**
	- Not caused by left TPJ damage - by unknown reason
- ALSO **superior/middle temporal lobe / ventro-lateral PFC** damage
- Symptoms:
    - Ignore contralateral space
    - Drawing bias
    - Impaired attention
#### 2. Gerstmann syndrome (Left parietal)
- **Left angular gyrus / supramaginal gyrus** damage
- Symptoms
	- Finger agnosia
	- Agraphia
	- Dyscalculia
	- Left-right disorientation
#### 3. Visuomotor deficits
- Parietal damage
	- Symptoms
		- **Constructional apraxia**  
	    → cannot copy drawings / 3D constructions
- **Right superior parietal damage**
	- Symptoms 
		- **Optic ataxia**  
	    → Visuomotor coordination deficit: cannot reach accurately using vision
    
### E. Somatosensory cortex
- Location: **Postcentral gyrus**
- BA: **1, 2, 3**
- Input: **Ventral posterior thalamus**
- Organization:
    - Somatotopic (homunculus)
### Secondary somatosensory cortex
- Deficit:
    - **Astereognosis**  
        → Tactile agnosia: cannot recognize objects by touch

	- Connected to PFC

### Lesions
- Alien limb syndrome
- No spontaneous movement

## D. Mirror neurons
- Found in:
    - **Premotor + parietal cortex**
- Function:
	- Matching self actions with actions performed by others
    - Action understanding
    - Social learning

# Example Question


## 1. “Compare and contrast the roles of the parietal and temporal cortices in sensory processing and behaviour.”

The cerebral cortex is organised into specialised regions that process different aspects of sensory information and guide behaviour. Among these, the parietal and temporal cortices play complementary roles in transforming sensory input into perception and action. While the parietal cortex is primarily involved in <font color="#c00000">spatial processing and action guidance</font>, the temporal cortex is responsible for <font color="#c00000">object recognition and linking perception to memory</font>. Together, they form key components of the dorsal and ventral visual processing streams.

The parietal cortex is a central component of the dorsal visual stream, often referred to as the “where” or “how” pathway. This pathway originates in the primary visual cortex and projects to the parietal lobe. The parietal cortex integrates<font color="#c00000"> visual, somatosensory, and proprioceptive information</font> to construct a <font color="#c00000">representation of space relative to the body</font>. This allows the brain to guide movements based on visual input, such as reaching or grasping objects. In addition to its role in<font color="#c00000"> visuomotor coordination</font>, the parietal cortex is critical for attention, particularly <font color="#c00000">spatial attention</font>. It enables the <font color="#c00000">selection of relevant stimuli in the environment </font>and supports <font color="#c00000">goal-directed behaviour</font>.

Damage to the parietal cortex can result in profound deficits in <font color="#c00000">spatial awareness and action</font>. One of the most well-known conditions is <font color="#c00000">unilateral spatial neglect</font>, typically caused by lesions in the <font color="#c00000">right temporoparietal junction or inferior parietal lobule</font>. Patients with this condition fail to attend to stimuli on the contralateral side of space, despite having intact sensory systems. Other deficits include <font color="#c00000">optic ataxia</font>, in which patients have difficulty using visual information to guide movements, and <font color="#c00000">constructional apraxia</font>, reflecting impaired spatial organisation.

In contrast, the temporal cortex is a key component of the ventral visual stream, known as the “what” pathway. This pathway also begins in the primary visual cortex but projects to the temporal lobe. The temporal cortex processes<font color="#c00000"> detailed features</font> of visual stimuli, such as <font color="#c00000">shape, colour, and texture</font>, allowing objects to be identified and categorised. It also plays a critical role in linking sensory information to stored knowledge and memory. Specialised regions within the temporal cortex include the <font color="#c00000">fusiform face area</font>, which is involved in <font color="#c00000">face recognition</font>, and the <font color="#c00000">parahippocampal place area</font>, which processes <font color="#c00000">scenes and spatial contexts</font>.

Lesions of the temporal cortex primarily affect object recognition. A classic example is <font color="#c00000">prosopagnosia</font>, in which individuals are unable to recognise familiar faces despite normal vision. More broadly, damage can lead to <font color="#c00000">visual agnosia</font>, where patients cannot identify objects even though they can perceive them. These deficits highlight the importance of the temporal cortex in assigning meaning to sensory input.

Although the parietal and temporal cortices have distinct functions, they operate as part of an integrated system. The dorsal and ventral streams interact to allow coordinated perception and action. For example, recognising an object (ventral stream) and reaching for it (dorsal stream) require continuous communication between these pathways. Furthermore, both regions are connected to higher-order areas such as the prefrontal cortex, which integrates sensory information with goals and decision-making processes.

In summary, the parietal cortex is specialised for spatial processing and action guidance, while the temporal cortex is responsible for object recognition and memory integration. Their complementary roles within the dorsal and ventral streams enable the brain to construct a coherent representation of the environment and generate appropriate behaviour.



## 2. “Give an outline of the functional organisation of the cerebral cortex, including large-scale networks and their roles in behaviour.”

The cerebral cortex is organised into a highly complex system of specialised regions and large-scale networks that work together to support perception, action, and cognition. Rather than functioning as isolated areas, cortical regions interact through distributed networks that integrate information across multiple domains. This organisation allows the brain to process sensory input, generate behaviour, and adapt to changing environments.

At a broad level, the cortex is divided into functional regions associated with sensory processing, motor control, and higher cognitive functions. Sensory areas, such as the visual cortex in the occipital lobe, receive input from the thalamus and process basic features of stimuli. Motor areas in the frontal lobe, including the primary motor cortex, premotor cortex, and supplementary motor area, are responsible for planning and executing voluntary movements. Association areas, particularly in the parietal, temporal, and prefrontal cortices, integrate information from multiple modalities and support complex cognitive functions.

One of the most important organisational principles of the cortex is the presence of parallel processing streams. For example, visual information is processed through dorsal and ventral pathways, which handle spatial and object-related information, respectively. Similarly, language processing involves both dorsal and ventral streams, linking sound processing to motor articulation and semantic understanding. These parallel pathways allow the brain to process different aspects of information simultaneously.

Beyond regional specialisation, the cortex is organised into <font color="#c00000">large-scale functional networks</font>. These networks consist of anatomically distributed regions that are functionally connected and co-activated during specific tasks. One key network is the<font color="#c00000"> default mode network</font>, which is active during rest and internally directed thought. In contrast, the<font color="#c00000"> dorsal attention network </font>is involved in <font color="#c00000">goal-directed, top-down attention</font>, allowing individuals to focus on relevant stimuli. The <font color="#c00000">ventral attention network </font>responds to<font color="#c00000"> unexpected or salient stimuli and can redirect attention away from ongoing tasks</font>.

Another important network is the <font color="#c00000">fronto-parietal or central executive network</font>, which supports higher cognitive functions such as working memory, problem-solving, and decision-making. This network includes regions of the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and posterior parietal cortex and is essential for flexible, goal-directed behaviour. These networks interact dynamically, with activity shifting depending on task demands.

The prefrontal cortex plays a central role in integrating information across these networks. It receives input from sensory and limbic regions and is involved in planning, decision-making, and regulating behaviour. Subregions of the prefrontal cortex, such as the orbitofrontal cortex, are particularly important for evaluating the emotional significance of stimuli and guiding appropriate responses.

Importantly, disruptions to these networks can lead to cognitive and behavioural deficits. For example, damage to the dorsal attention network can impair the ability to focus attention, while dysfunction in the ventral attention network can lead to neglect. Similarly, abnormalities in the fronto-parietal network are associated with deficits in executive function.

In summary, the cerebral cortex is organised into specialised regions and interconnected networks that support a wide range of functions. Through parallel processing and dynamic interactions between networks, the cortex enables flexible and adaptive behaviour. This distributed organisation is a key feature of human brain function and underlies our ability to perceive, think, and act in complex environments.
