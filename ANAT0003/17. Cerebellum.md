## 1.1 Surface Features

- Highly folded cortex → folds called **folia**
- Midline structure between hemispheres → **Vermis**
- Flocculonodular lobe = **Flocculus + Nodule**
- Primary fissure divides: anterior lobe, posterior lobe

## 1.2 Brainstem Attachment

Connected to brainstem via **three cerebellar peduncles**:
- Inferior peduncle
- Middle peduncle
- Superior peduncle
These carry all afferent and efferent fibres.

# 3. Deep Cerebellar Nuclei

- Located within the white matter deep to the cerebellar cortex. 'Output stations' of the cerebellum.
- 4 bilaterally paired sets of deep cerebellar nuclei.
- Different deep nuclei are connected with different parts of the cerebellar cortex, and are part of different functional zone circuits.

<img src="assets/Pasted%20image%2020260303003828.png" alt="Pasted image 20260303003828">

| Nucleus    | Connected Region         | Functional Division                   |
| ---------- | ------------------------ | ------------------------------------- |
| Fastigial  | Vermis + Flocculonodular | Vestibulocerebellum + Spinocerebellum |
| Emboliform | Paravermis               | Spinocerebellum                       |
| Globose    | Paravermis               | Spinocerebellum                       |
| Dentate    | Lateral lobes            | Pontocerebellum                       |

- Globose + Emboliform = Interposed nucleus (hard to separate)
- Dentate = largest and visible grossly

# 4. Intra-cerebellar Circuit

<img src="assets/Pasted%20image%2020260303004443.png" width="425" alt="Pasted image 20260303004443">

## 4.1 Two types of input 

### 1. mossy fibres

#### Origins: 

Arise in many afferent structures, depending on functional zone.
- Spinocerebellar tracts (information from somatosensory and proprioceptive receptors)
- Pontine nuclei
- Vestibular nuclei
- Reticular formation

#### Properties:

- Contact granule cells
- Also send collaterals to deep nuclei

### 2. climbing fibres

#### Origin:

- Contralateral **Inferior olive only**

#### Properties:

- One climbing fibre per Purkinje cell
- Hundreds of synapses
- Generates **complex spikes**
- Also send collaterals to deep nuclei

## 4.2 Cortical output

- Purkinje cells are the **only output of cerebellar cortex**
- Neurotransmitter: **GABA**
- Effect: **Purely inhibitory**
- Target: Deep cerebellar nuclei
Deep nuclei then send excitatory output to efferent structures.

# 5. Cortical Structure

<img src="assets/Pasted%20image%2020260303005405.png" width="525" alt="Pasted image 20260303005405">

## 5.1 Three Layers

1. Molecular layer
    - Mainly neuropil (axons and dendrites), few cell bodies. Where neurons communicate
    - Parallel fibres
    - Interneurons (stellate, basket)
2. Purkinje layer
    - 2–3 cell thin
    - Very large Purkinje cell cell bodies
3. Granule layer
    - Extremely dense
    - ~60 billion granule cells
    - Very small granule cell cell bodies

## 5.2 Purkinje cells

- Flat dendritic tree (orthogonal to folium)
- Each receives:
    - 1 climbing fibre, hundreds of synapses (strong, specific, complex spikes)
    - Thousands of parallel fibre inputs (weak individually, broad and distributed)

## 5.3 Climbing fibres

 - One climbing fibre AP reliably fires a burst of Purkinje cell spikes (complex spikes)
 - Each Inferior Olive cell sends climbing fibre efferents to a small number (5-10) of Purkinje cells

## 5.4 Granule Cells

- Each mossy fibres connects to many granule cells (10s - 100s) 
- Each granule cell has 1 parallel fibre
- Each parallel fibre synapses on many Purkinje cells
- Each parrallel fibre makes 1 - 2 synapses with each Purkinje cell → weak and distributed coding

## 5.5 Local interneurons

| Interneuron | Target             |
| ----------- | ------------------ |
| Stellate    | Purkinje dendrites |
| Basket      | Purkinje soma      |
| Golgi       | Granule dendrites  |
All have inhibition effects.

### Function:

- Sharpen spatial zones of excitation

## 6.1 Pontocerebellum

- Origin of pontocerebellar mossy fibres 
- Through pontine nuclei in midbrain
- Lateral parts of anterior and posterior lobes

### Input

Cortex → Pontine nuclei → (via **middle peduncle**) → Contralateral cerebellum  

#### Cortical sources:

All neocortical areas except V1
- Motor cortex
- Premotor cortex
- Prefrontal
- Temporal
- Parietal
Pontine nuclei with shared cortical afferents grouped in zones within pons

### Output

Main: Dentate nucleus → (via **superior peduncle**) → Ventral lateral thalamus → M1  
Collaterals: Dentate nucleus → (via superior peduncle) → Red nucleus → Inferior olivary nucleus

### Pontocerebellum motor control circuit

Motor cortex → Pons → Cerebellum → Thalamus → Motor cortex

- Inferior olive acts as a powerful position to modulate activity in that loop. It has direct access to somatosensory information for feedback following motor commands.
- Double decussation → any damage to cerebellum produces ipsilateral deficits

### Effects of pontocerebellum dysfunction

Cerebellar hemispheric syndrome
- Lack of coordination of voluntary movements (overshoot/undershoot of reaching)
- Decomposition of normally smooth movement (jerky, uncoordinated stepwise movements)
- Intention tremor (uncontrolled oscillation when attempting a precise movement)
- Speech affected

## 6.3 Vestibulocerebellum  

- vestibulocerebelar cortex = flocculonodular lobe
- Flocculonodular Purkinje cells project directly to vestibular nuclei and also indirectly via the fastigial nucleus

### Input

- Vestibular system → vestibular nuclei (in brainstem)
- Visual areas (pretectal area, visual cortex)

### Output

- Reticular formation → reticulospinal tract
- Vestibular nuclei → vestibulospinal tract / oculomotor nuclei (cranial nerve III)

# 7. Functions of cerebellum

## Motor learning

Cerebellum has a highly plastic learning system by sensory-motor mismatch. The learning is unconscious.

### Physiological basis of motor learning

- Specific parallel fibre activity + climbing fibre activation →  **Long-Term Depression (LTD)** at PF–Purkinje synapse (to PF input)
- This process changes synaptic weight

<img src="assets/Pasted%20image%2020260303180453.png" width="275" alt="Pasted image 20260303180453">

During learning:
- Climbing fibre activity present  
After adaptation:
- Climbing fibre activity disappears

## Cerebellum and cognition

### Evidence for cognitive role:

- Reciprocal anatomical connections with:
    - Prefrontal cortex
    - Parietal cortex
    - Temporal cortex
- fMRI activation in:
    - Working memory
    - Attention
    - Language
    - Executive control
    - Emotion
- Cerebellar affective syndrome

### Counterarguments:

- fMRI may reflect motor planning or internal verbalization relating to task
- High variability between patients

### Current consensus:  

→ Cerebellum likely has cognitive roles  
→ Exact extent unresolved

# Example Question

## Overview

The cerebellum is a highly organised brain structure located posterior to the brainstem, playing a crucial role in motor coordination, balance, and motor learning. Although it does not initiate movement, it <font color="#c00000">refines motor output </font>by comparing <font color="#c00000">intended actions</font> with <font color="#c00000">actual performance </font>and generating <font color="#c00000">corrective signals</font>. Its organisation reflects a tight coupling between anatomical structure, internal circuitry, and functional output .

Anatomically, the cerebellum consists of two hemispheres separated by a midline structure known as the **vermis**, and a highly folded cortical surface forming **folia**, which greatly increase surface area. It is divided into three lobes: the anterior lobe, posterior lobe, and the **flocculonodular lobe**, the latter being evolutionarily oldest. The cerebellum is connected to the brainstem via three cerebellar peduncles: inferior, middle, and superior, which carry afferent and efferent fibres .

Functionally, the cerebellum is divided into three major regions based on input–output connectivity. The **vestibulocerebellum** (flocculonodular lobe) is involved in balance and eye movements; the **spinocerebellum** (vermis and paravermis) regulates posture and ongoing movements; and the **pontocerebellum** (lateral hemispheres) is associated with motor planning and coordination, particularly in connection with the neocortex. These divisions also correspond to evolutionary stages, from the archeocerebellum to the neocerebellum.

Deep within the cerebellar white matter lie the **deep cerebellar nuclei**, which act as the main output stations. There are four paired nuclei: the<font color="#c00000"> fastigial, globose, emboliform, and dentate nuclei</font>. Each is associated with specific cortical regions and functional divisions. For example, the fastigial nucleus is linked to the vermis and flocculonodular lobe, while the dentate nucleus, the largest, is associated with the lateral hemispheres . The globose and emboliform nuclei together form the interposed nucleus.

The internal circuitry of the cerebellum is highly uniform and consists of two main types of<font color="#c00000"> input</font>: **mossy fibres** and **climbing fibres**. Mossy fibres originate from multiple sources, including <font color="#c00000">spinocerebellar tracts, pontine nuclei, vestibular nuclei, and the reticular formation</font>. They synapse onto <font color="#c00000">granule cells</font>, which in turn give rise to <font color="#c00000">parallel fibres</font> that synapse broadly onto Purkinje cells. In contrast, climbing fibres originate exclusively from the **inferior olive** and form powerful, specific connections with Purkinje cells, generating <font color="#c00000">complex spikes</font> . Each Purkinje cell receives input from a single climbing fibre but from thousands of parallel fibres, enabling both precise and distributed coding.

The cerebellar cortex consists of three layers: the molecular layer, the Purkinje cell layer, and the granule cell layer. **Purkinje cells are the sole output neurons of the cerebellar cortex** except for collaterals from mossy fibres and climbing fibres, and they are <font color="#c00000">inhibitory (GABAergic)</font>, projecting to the deep cerebellar nuclei. These nuclei then provide excitatory output to other brain regions . <font color="#c00000">Local interneurons, including stellate, basket, and Golgi cells</font>, provide inhibitory modulation, <font color="#c00000">sharpening spatial patterns of activity</font>.

The cerebellum operates through distinct functional circuits. The **pontocerebellum** receives input from the cerebral cortex via the<font color="#c00000"> pontine nuclei</font> and <font color="#c00000">middle cerebellar peduncle</font>, and sends output <font color="#c00000">via the dentate nucleus to the thalamus and back to motor cortex</font>, forming a loop involved in <font color="#c00000">motor planning</font>. The **spinocerebellum** receives <font color="#c00000">proprioceptive input</font> from the body through various<font color="#c00000"> spinocerebellar tracts </font>and integrates this with <font color="#c00000">motor commands</font> to adjust ongoing movements. Its output targets include the <font color="#c00000">red nucleus and vestibular nuclei</font>, influencing motor execution via descending pathways. The **vestibulocerebellum** receives input from the <font color="#c00000">vestibular system</font> and <font color="#c00000">visual areas</font> and projects to <font color="#c00000">vestibular nuclei and reticular formation</font>, regulating balance, posture, and eye movements, including the <font color="#c00000">vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR)</font> .

Functionally, the cerebellum is essential for coordination, timing, and precision of movement. It integrates motor commands with sensory feedback, particularly proprioceptive information, to ensure smooth execution of voluntary actions. It also supports rhythmic and coordinated movements, posture control, and gaze stabilisation. A key feature of cerebellar function is **motor learning**, which depends on synaptic plasticity mechanisms such as <font color="#c00000">long-term depression at parallel fibre–Purkinje cell synapses</font>. This learning is driven by <font color="#c00000">sensory–motor mismatch signals conveyed by climbing fibres</font> .

Clinically, cerebellar damage produces characteristic deficits. Because of double decussation in its pathways, lesions result in **ipsilateral motor deficits**. These include ataxia, intention tremor, dysmetria (overshoot/undershoot), and decomposition of movement. Midline lesions affect posture and gait, while hemispheric lesions impair voluntary limb movements. Additionally, emerging evidence suggests a role for the cerebellum in cognition, with connections to prefrontal and parietal cortices and involvement in functions such as working memory, attention, and language.

In summary, the cerebellum is a highly structured system that integrates sensory inputs and motor commands through specialised circuits and outputs corrective signals, enabling precise, coordinated, and adaptive behaviour.
