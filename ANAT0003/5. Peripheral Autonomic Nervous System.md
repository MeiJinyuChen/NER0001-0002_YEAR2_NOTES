
# 1. General ANS
## 1.1 Core definition and organization
- Regulates **internal homeostasis**
- Innervates:
    - Visceral organs
    - Smooth muscle
    - Secretory glands
- **Not under voluntary control**
- Always **2-neuron chain**:
    - Preganglionic neuron (CNS → ganglion)
    - Postganglionic neuron (ganglion → target)
## 1.2 Divisions of ANS

### (1) Sympathetic (SNS) 
- Origin: **Spinal cord (thoracic segments)**
- Structure: **Simple, chain-like ganglia**
### (2) Parasympathetic (PNS) 
- Origin:
    - **Brainstem (cranial nerves)**
    - **Sacral spinal cord (S2–S4)**
- Structure: **More diffuse, ganglia near organs**
### (3) Enteric Nervous System (ENS)
- “**Gut brain**”
- Semi-independent system controlling GI tract
## 1.2 Neurotransmitters

| Location                       | Neurotransmitter   |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------ |
| Preganglionic (both SNS & PNS) | ACh                |
| Postganglionic SNS             | Noradrenaline (NE) |
| Postganglionic PNS             | ACh                |

Exception:
- **Sympathetic → sweat glands = cholinergic (ACh)**

# 3. ANS structure
## 3.1 SNS structure

- Origin: **Spinal cord (thoracic segments)**
- Structure: **Simple, chain-like ganglia**
- Sympathetic ganglia can be divided by:
  Paravertebral ganglia (cervical ganglia)
  Prevertebral ganglia (coeliac, mesentric ganglia)

| Ganglion            | Organs                                                                                        | Spinal Level |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------ |
| Superior cervical   | Head & neck, blood vessels (cutaneous), sweat glands, erector pili                            | T1–T4        |
| Coeliac             | Foregut (oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, liver, biliary system) and vascular structures | T5–T9        |
| Superior mesenteric | Midgut (large intestine) and vascular structures                                              | T10–T11      |
| Inferior mesenteric | Hindgut (large intestine, kidney, bladder, reproductive organs) and vascular structures       | L1–L2        |
Notice: 
Adrenal gland is the only organ that directly connect to the pre-ganglionic neuron (thoracic splanchnic nerve).
- The medulla of the adrenal gland is the homologue of a sympathetic ganglion, being derived from the neural crest.
### Lateral horn
- Contains cell bodies of preganglionic sympathetic neurons (the first neuron in the sympathetic pathway)
- Axon exits via:
    - Ventral root → Spinal nerve → White ramus communicans
- Sympathetic preganglionic neurons originate from the lateral horn of T1–L2 spinal cord segments. Their axons leave via the ventral root and spinal nerve, then <font color="#c00000">enter the sympathetic chain through white rami communicantes</font>. Within the sympathetic chain, fibres may synapse at the same level, ascend or descend before synapsing, or pass through without synapsing to form splanchnic nerves. <font color="#c00000">Postganglionic fibres leave sympathetic ganglia via grey rami communicantes</font> to supply body wall structures (sweat glands, blood vessels of skin, arrector pili muscles), or via visceral branches to supply internal organs through prevertebral ganglia and plexuses.

# 4. Descending Modulation of ANS

### 1. Cortical 
- Cognitive modulation
- Functionally active at the same time as the SNS
1. Medial prefrontal cortex
2. Anterior parts of the insula & cingulate gyrus
### 2. Hypothalamus (Homeostasis)
- Homeostasis modulation
1. Hypothalamic signals reach autonomic nuclei in the brainstem and spinal cord directly
2. Through relays in the reticular formation
### 3. Limbic 
- Emotional modulation
- Direct projections
1. Amygdala
2. Septal area
### 4. Visceral nuclei
- Visceral modulation
1. Solitary nucleus (in medulla)
2. PAG (in midbrain)

# 5. Enteric Nervous System (ENS)

### Structure
- Myenteric plexus
- Submucosal plexus
### Key properties
- ~100 million neurons
- Has:
    - Intrinsic primary afferent neurons (IPANs)
    - Local inhibitor interneurons
	    - Together, can function independently of CNS. Controls:
		    - Peristalsis
		    - Secretion
- Communicate with CNS bidirectionally (90%  vagal fibres between gut and brain = afferent) : 
	- Sensations of nausea, satiety -- influencing mood
- Communicate with many other cell types (e.g. intestinal epithelials, endocrine cells, immune cells) 
	- Influence physiological gut responses

# 6. Visceral Afferents 

*Note: Not part of ANS (ANS = efferent only)*
## Types

### (1) Physiological afferents
- Travel with **parasympathetic**
- Includes:
    - Chemoreceptors and baroreceptors
    - Other general physiology of the viscera
- Participate in reflexes involved in the control of:
	- Circulation
	- Respiration
	- Digestion
	- Micturition
	- Coition
### (2) Pain afferents
- Travel with **sympathetic**
- Then synapse in dorsal horn
## Referred Pain
- Due to shared pathways between visceral and somatic pain
- Example:
    - Cardiac pain → chest + left arm

# Example Question


## 1. Briefly describe the anatomical organization and functional differences between the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems.

The autonomic nervous system (ANS) is responsible for involuntary regulation of internal organs, maintaining physiological homeostasis through coordinated control of cardiac muscle, smooth muscle, and glands. It is divided into the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) and parasympathetic nervous system (PNS), which differ anatomically, neurochemically, and functionally.

Both SNS and PNS pathways share a common two-neuron organization. <font color="#c00000">Preganglionic neurons originate in the CNS and synapse in autonomic ganglia</font>, where postganglionic neurons project to target tissues. In both divisions, preganglionic neurons release acetylcholine (ACh) onto nicotinic receptors.

The SNS originates from<font color="#c00000"> thoracolumbar spinal cord segments T1–L2</font>, specifically from neurons located in the <font color="#c00000">lateral horn</font>. Preganglionic fibres <font color="#c00000">exit through ventral roots</font>,then enter the sympathetic chain through white rami communicantes. Within the sympathetic chain, fibres may synapse at the same level, ascend or descend before synapsing, or pass through without synapsing to form splanchnic nerves. Postganglionic fibres leave sympathetic ganglia via grey rami communicantes to supply body wall structures (sweat glands, blood vessels of skin, arrector pili muscles), or via visceral branches to supply internal organs through prevertebral ganglia and plexuses.

Sympathetic postganglionic neurons usually release noradrenaline (NE) onto adrenergic receptors. Exceptions include sympathetic innervation of<font color="#c00000"> sweat glands</font>, which uses ACh. The adrenal medulla represents a modified sympathetic ganglion, directly innervated by preganglionic neurons and releasing adrenaline and noradrenaline into circulation.

Functionally, the SNS mediates “fight or flight” responses, increasing heart rate, blood pressure, bronchodilation, pupil dilation, and skeletal muscle perfusion while suppressing gastrointestinal activity.

By contrast, the PNS originates from<font color="#c00000"> craniosacral regions</font>: brainstem nuclei associated with <font color="#c00000">cranial nerves III, VII, IX, and X</font>, as well as sacral spinal cord segments<font color="#c00000"> S2–S4</font>. Parasympathetic ganglia are located <font color="#c00000">near or within target organs</font>, resulting in <font color="#c00000">long preganglionic and short postganglionic fibres</font>. Both pre- and postganglionic neurons release ACh, with postganglionic neurons acting primarily via muscarinic receptors.
 
The PNS promotes “rest and digest” functions, decreasing heart rate, stimulating digestion, enhancing glandular secretion, constricting pupils, and supporting urination and reproductive processes. The vagus nerve is especially important, providing parasympathetic innervation to most thoracic and abdominal organs.

Thus, the SNS and PNS operate largely as complementary systems, balancing energy expenditure with conservation. Their structural differences—thoracolumbar versus craniosacral origins, ganglion placement, and neurotransmitter use—reflect their specialized physiological roles. Higher brain regions, especially the hypothalamus and limbic system, coordinate both divisions to integrate autonomic responses with emotional and environmental demands.


## 2. Give an overview of the enteric nervous system and descending CNS modulation of autonomic function.

The enteric nervous system (ENS) is a specialized subdivision of the peripheral autonomic nervous system responsible for regulating gastrointestinal function. Often termed the “gut brain,” the ENS possesses significant autonomy and can coordinate digestive activity independently of the central nervous system (CNS), although it remains modulated by sympathetic and parasympathetic inputs.

Structurally, the ENS is composed primarily of two interconnected plexuses: the myenteric (Auerbach’s) plexus, located between longitudinal and circular smooth muscle layers and primarily controlling motility, and the submucosal (Meissner’s) plexus, which regulates secretion and blood flow. Together, these networks contain approximately 100 million neurons, including intrinsic primary afferent neurons, interneurons, and motor neurons.

The ENS controls key gastrointestinal functions such as peristalsis, secretion, local vascular regulation, and reflex coordination. Because it contains intrinsic sensory circuits and local processing capacity, it can maintain many digestive functions even in the absence of direct CNS input.

Despite this autonomy, the CNS exerts descending modulation over the ENS and broader ANS through several hierarchical systems. At the cortical level, regions including the medial prefrontal cortex, anterior insula, and cingulate cortex contribute cognitive and emotional regulation of autonomic output. These areas are particularly important during stress or conscious emotional states.

The hypothalamus serves as the principal homeostatic regulator, integrating endocrine, visceral, and behavioral responses. Hypothalamic nuclei influence autonomic output directly via projections to brainstem and spinal autonomic nuclei or indirectly via the reticular formation.

Limbic structures, especially the amygdala and septal nuclei, provide emotional modulation of autonomic responses, linking fear, anxiety, and reward to physiological changes.

Brainstem visceral nuclei such as the solitary nucleus process incoming visceral sensory information, while the periaqueductal grey (PAG) coordinates autonomic and defensive responses.

Visceral afferents, although not technically part of the ANS, are essential for bidirectional communication between organs and CNS. Approximately 90% of vagal fibres are afferent, conveying satiety, nausea, and internal physiological status.

Overall, autonomic control represents a highly integrated system in which the ENS provides local digestive autonomy while descending CNS networks ensure broader coordination with behavior, homeostasis, and emotional state. This hierarchical organization allows flexible physiological adaptation to both internal and external demands.
