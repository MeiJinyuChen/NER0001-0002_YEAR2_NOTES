# 3. Motor Circuit

## 3.1 Direct Pathway (“GO”)

## Function

→ **Facilitates movement**

<img src="assets/Pasted%20image%2020260409131831.png" width="475" alt="Pasted image 20260409131831">

## Mechanism

1. DA release (SNpc) → excites striatum (D1)
2. D1 neurons → inhibits GPi
3. GPi normally inhibits thalamus
4. ↓ GPi inhibition → **VLN (thalamus) activated**
5.  VLN → excites motor cortex

- ↑ level of DA in straitum → movement

## 3.2 Indirect Pathway (“NO-GO”)

## Function

→ **Suppresses movement**

<img src="assets/Pasted%20image%2020260409132933.png" width="425" alt="Pasted image 20260409132933">

## Mechanism

1. DA release (SNpc) → excites striatum (D2)
2. D2 neurons → inhibits GPe
3. GPe normally inhibits STN
4. ↓ GPe → STN activated
5. STN → excites GPi
6. GPi → inhibits VLN
7. ↓ VLN → ↓ cortex activity → ↓ CST activity

- ↑ level of DA in straitum → Inhibits D2 neurons  
    → reduces indirect pathway  
    → promotes movement

## 3.3 Hyperdirect Pathway (“STOP”)

## Function

→ **Rapid stopping of actions**

<img src="assets/Pasted%20image%2020260409133248.png" width="425" alt="Pasted image 20260409133248">

## Mechanism

1. M1 → directly excites STN
2. STN → excites GPi
3. GPi → inhibits thalamus  
    → ↓ CST activity

- Faster than indirect pathway  
	→ “emergency brake”
- Not involve DA

**Note**
- Direct ≠ start, indirect ≠ stop
- BOTH D1 and D2 neurons active simultaneously with action onset
    → **action selection & competition**

# Example Question

## 1. “Briefly describe the direct, indirect and hyperdirect pathways through the basal ganglia and explain their roles in motor control.”

The basal ganglia are a group of interconnected subcortical nuclei that play a critical role in motor control by regulating the selection and initiation of actions. Rather than directly generating movement, they influence motor output by modulating thalamocortical activity. This modulation is achieved through three main pathways: the direct, indirect, and hyperdirect pathways, which together enable facilitation, suppression, and rapid stopping of movements.

A key organisational principle of the basal ganglia is that their output nuclei, primarily the internal segment of the globus pallidus (GPi) and the substantia nigra pars reticulata (SNpr), exert tonic inhibitory control over the thalamus. This means that, under resting conditions, thalamocortical activity is suppressed. Movement is therefore initiated by reducing this inhibition, a process known as disinhibition.

The direct pathway facilitates movement by reducing inhibitory output from the basal ganglia. It begins with excitatory input from the motor cortex to the striatum, specifically to neurons expressing D1 dopamine receptors. These striatal neurons send inhibitory projections to the GPi. Because the GPi normally inhibits the thalamus, inhibition of the GPi leads to reduced thalamic inhibition. As a result, the thalamus becomes more active and sends excitatory signals back to the motor cortex, promoting movement. Thus, the net effect of the direct pathway is to increase motor activity.

In contrast, the indirect pathway suppresses movement by increasing inhibitory output to the thalamus. Cortical input activates striatal neurons expressing D2 dopamine receptors, which project to the external segment of the globus pallidus (GPe). The GPe normally inhibits the subthalamic nucleus (STN). When the GPe is inhibited, the STN becomes more active and sends excitatory projections to the GPi. This increases GPi activity, leading to stronger inhibition of the thalamus and reduced cortical excitation. The net effect is suppression of movement.

The hyperdirect pathway provides a mechanism for rapid inhibition of movement. In this pathway, the cortex sends direct excitatory input to the STN, bypassing the striatum. The STN then excites the GPi, which in turn increases inhibition of the thalamus. Because this pathway involves fewer synapses, it acts more quickly than the indirect pathway and is thought to function as an “emergency brake” to stop actions that are inappropriate or need to be rapidly terminated.

Dopamine, released from the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNpc), plays a crucial modulatory role in these pathways. It enhances the activity of the direct pathway by exciting D1 receptor-expressing neurons and suppresses the indirect pathway by inhibiting D2 receptor-expressing neurons. Both effects act to promote movement, highlighting the importance of dopamine in motor control.

Importantly, these pathways do not operate independently. Both direct and indirect pathway neurons are active simultaneously during movement, contributing to a process of action selection and competition rather than simple on-off control. This allows the basal ganglia to facilitate desired movements while suppressing competing alternatives.

In summary, the basal ganglia regulate motor activity through a balance of facilitation and inhibition mediated by the direct, indirect, and hyperdirect pathways. The direct pathway promotes movement, the indirect pathway suppresses it, and the hyperdirect pathway provides rapid stopping. Together, these pathways ensure smooth, coordinated, and context-appropriate motor behaviour.

## 2. “Describe the functional organisation of basal ganglia circuits and explain how they link motor, cognitive, and limbic processes.”

The basal ganglia are organised into a series of parallel functional circuits that link the cerebral cortex with subcortical structures and back to the cortex via the thalamus. These circuits are not limited to motor control but also play important roles in cognition and emotion. The functional organisation of these circuits allows the basal ganglia to integrate information across different domains and contribute to action selection, decision-making, and reward-based learning.

A fundamental feature of basal ganglia organisation is the presence of cortico-basal ganglia-thalamocortical loops. In each loop, information flows from specific regions of the cortex to the striatum, then through the basal ganglia output nuclei (GPi or SNpr), to the thalamus, and finally back to the cortex. Each loop is largely segregated, meaning that different types of information are processed in parallel.

The <font color="#c00000">motor loop</font> involves projections from motor areas of the cortex, including the primary motor cortex, supplementary motor area, and premotor cortex, to the putamen. From the <font color="#c00000">putamen</font>, <font color="#c00000">information is processed through the direct and indirect pathways of the basal ganglia </font>and then relayed via the <font color="#c00000">ventrolateral (VL) nucleus </font>of the thalamus back to motor cortical areas. This loop is responsible for the planning and execution of voluntary movements and ensures that appropriate motor programs are selected and competing movements are suppressed.

The<font color="#c00000"> c<font color="#c00000">ognitive or associative loop</font></font> involves projections from the prefrontal cortex to the caudate nucleus. The caudate processes information related to <font color="#c00000">executive functions such as planning, working memory, and decision-making</font>. Output from this circuit passes through the basal ganglia and is relayed via the<font color="#c00000"> ventral anterior (VA) </font>nucleus of the thalamus back to the prefrontal cortex. This loop allows the basal ganglia to influence higher-order cognitive processes and behavioural flexibility.

The <font color="#c00000">limbic loop</font> involves projections from <font color="#c00000">limbic regions</font>, including the orbitofrontal cortex and ventromedial prefrontal cortex, to the<font color="#c00000"> nucleus accumbens</font>, which is part of the ventral striatum. This circuit is closely associated with reward processing, motivation, and emotional behaviour. <font color="#c00000">Dopaminergic input from the ventral tegmental area (VTA) plays a key role in this loop</font>, particularly in encoding <font color="#c00000">reward prediction and reinforcing learning</font>. Output from the nucleus accumbens is transmitted through the ventral pallidum to the thalamus and then back to cortical regions.

These three loops demonstrate how the basal ganglia integrate motor, cognitive, and emotional information. Although they are anatomically distinct, they interact functionally, allowing information from one domain to influence another. For example, reward signals processed in the limbic loop can influence decision-making in the cognitive loop and ultimately affect motor output.

In addition to their role in <font color="#c00000">real-time action selection</font>, basal ganglia circuits are involved in <font color="#c00000">learning</font>. The caudate nucleus is associated with <font color="#c00000">goal-directed learning</font>, where actions are performed based on expected outcomes, while the putamen is associated with <font color="#c00000">habit learning</font>, where <font color="#c00000">actions become automatic through repetition</font>. Dopamine plays a critical role in this process by signalling reward prediction errors and reinforcing successful behaviours.

Clinically, disruption of these circuits can lead to a range of disorders affecting movement, cognition, and emotion. For example, Parkinson’s disease primarily affects the motor loop, while disorders such as addiction involve dysfunction of the limbic loop.

In summary, the basal ganglia are organised into parallel circuits that link the cortex, basal ganglia, and thalamus. These circuits integrate motor, cognitive, and limbic functions, enabling the selection and execution of appropriate behaviours in a complex and dynamic environment.

## 3. Overview

The basal ganglia are a group of subcortical nuclei that play a central role in motor control, action selection, learning, and motivation. They do not directly generate movement but instead regulate the initiation, suppression, and scaling of motor and cognitive actions through distributed loops with the cortex and thalamus. Anatomically, the basal ganglia can be defined narrowly as the **cerebral nuclei**, including the **striatum (caudate nucleus and putamen)** and the **globus pallidus**, or more broadly as a functional circuit incorporating the **subthalamic nucleus (STN)**, **substantia nigra (SN)**, and **ventral tegmental area (VTA)**. Although the **thalamus is not part of the basal ganglia**, it is an essential relay within these circuits .

The striatum serves as the primary input structure and consists of the caudate nucleus and putamen, which are functionally similar but differ in connectivity. The globus pallidus is divided into an **external segment (GPe)**, which participates in intermediate processing, and an **internal segment (GPi)**, which acts as a major output nucleus. Additional output is provided by the **substantia nigra pars reticulata (SNpr)**, which is functionally equivalent to GPi. In contrast, the **substantia nigra pars compacta (SNpc)** provides dopaminergic input to the striatum, modulating activity. A key feature of basal ganglia organisation is that **most projection neurons are GABAergic**, meaning that basal ganglia output is predominantly inhibitory .

At the cellular level, the striatum contains two major populations of projection neurons distinguished by their dopamine receptor expression. **D1 receptor-expressing neurons** form the **direct pathway**, while **D2 receptor-expressing neurons** form the **indirect pathway**. Dopamine has opposite effects on these pathways: it excites D1 neurons via Gs-coupled receptors and inhibits D2 neurons via Gi-coupled receptors, thereby promoting movement overall .

The basal ganglia operate through three main pathways. The **direct pathway (“GO” pathway)** facilitates movement. In this pathway, dopaminergic input from the SNpc excites D1 neurons in the striatum, which then inhibit the GPi. Because the GPi normally exerts tonic inhibition on the thalamus, its inhibition leads to disinhibition of the ventrolateral thalamic nucleus, allowing it to excite the motor cortex and promote movement . In contrast, the **indirect pathway (“NO-GO” pathway)** suppresses movement. Here, D2 neurons inhibit the GPe, which normally inhibits the STN. Reduced GPe activity leads to increased STN activity, which excites the GPi. This enhances GPi inhibition of the thalamus, reducing cortical activation and suppressing movement. Dopamine inhibits this pathway by suppressing D2 neuron activity, again favouring movement.

A third pathway, the **hyperdirect pathway (“STOP” pathway)**, provides rapid inhibition of movement. In this pathway, the motor cortex directly excites the STN, which then activates the GPi, leading to increased inhibition of the thalamus and immediate suppression of motor output. This pathway acts as an “emergency brake” and does not depend on dopamine . Importantly, both direct and indirect pathways are active simultaneously at movement onset, suggesting that the basal ganglia function not simply as on/off switches but as systems for **action selection and competition**.

The main outputs of the basal ganglia arise from the **GPi and SNpr**, which exert tonic inhibitory control over thalamic and brainstem targets. The SNpr also contributes to **oculomotor control**, projecting to the superior colliculus and regulating gaze. Through these outputs, the basal ganglia influence motor cortex activity via thalamocortical loops and can also control motor pattern generators independently of the cortex .

Functionally, the basal ganglia are organised into parallel loops linking different cortical areas to specific striatal regions. The **putamen-based loop** is associated with motor control, involving inputs from motor and premotor cortices and projections back via the ventrolateral thalamus. The **caudate-based loop** is involved in cognition and executive function, receiving input from the prefrontal cortex and projecting via the ventral anterior thalamus. The **limbic loop**, centred on the **nucleus accumbens (ventral striatum)**, integrates emotional and reward-related information, receiving inputs from the orbitofrontal cortex, ventromedial prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, and amygdala, and modulated by dopaminergic input from the VTA . Dopamine in this pathway encodes reward prediction and motivation (“wanting”) rather than pleasure itself, linking cognition, action, and emotion.

The basal ganglia also play a key role in learning. The caudate is involved in **goal-directed, reward-based learning**, while the putamen supports **habit learning**, characterised by automatic stimulus–response associations. This transition from goal-directed to habitual behaviour may involve dopaminergic signalling from the SNpc, effectively “chunking” actions into automatic sequences .

Clinically, dysfunction of basal ganglia circuits leads to characteristic movement disorders. In **Parkinson’s disease**, degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the SNpc leads to reduced direct pathway activity and increased indirect pathway activity, resulting in excessive inhibition of movement. This manifests as bradykinesia, rigidity, and tremor. In **hemiballismus**, lesions of the STN reduce indirect pathway activity, leading to excessive movement. In **Huntington’s disease**, degeneration of striatal neurons disrupts the balance of pathways, causing uncontrolled choreiform movements .

In summary, the basal ganglia are a highly organised network of inhibitory circuits that regulate motor, cognitive, and emotional processes through parallel loops and dynamic modulation of cortical activity.
