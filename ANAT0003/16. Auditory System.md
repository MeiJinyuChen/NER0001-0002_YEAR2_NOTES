# 2. Peripheral Auditory System

## 2.1 Outer Ear
### Structure
- Pinna
- Concha
- External auditory canal
- Eardrum = tympanic membrane
### Functions
1. **Sound amplification**
    - Pinna collects and funnels sound to eardrum
2. **Sound localisation**
    - Direction-dependent filtering (spectral cues)
## 2.2 Middle Ear
### Structure
![[Pasted image 20260412062740.png|450]]
- Ossicles: **malleus, incus, stapes**
- Eustachian tube
### Core Problem
- **Impedance mismatch** (air → cochlear fluid)
    - Without correction: 99.9% sound reflection 
### Solution: Pressure Amplification
Pressure=Force / Area
- Mechanisms:
	1. **Lever action of ossicles** → ↑ force
	2. **Area difference** (large eardrum vs small stapes footplate) → ↑ pressure
### Additional Function
![[Pasted image 20260412063613.png]]
- **Stapedius reflex** 
    - Activated by loud sounds
    - Reduces transmission → **protect the system from loud sound**

## 2.3 Inner Ear (Cochlea)

### 2.3.1 Basilar Membrane Mechanics 
![[Pasted image 20260412063822.png|250]]
#### Tonotopy
- **Base (stiff, narrow)** → high frequency
- **Apex (flexible, wide)** → low frequency
##### Governing Relationship
- ↑ stiffness (k) → ↑ frequency
- ↓ mass (m) → ↑ frequency

### 2.3.2 Hair Cell Transduction 
#### Structures
- Inner hair cells (IHCs) → main sensory output
- Outer hair cells (OHCs) → amplification
- Stereocilia + tip links
![[Pasted image 20260412064502.png|300]]
#### Mechanism
1. Basilar membrane movement → stereocilia deflection
2. Tip link tension changes
3. Opens (or close) **stretch-sensitive K⁺ channels**
4. K⁺ influx → depolarisation
5. Opens **voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels**
6. Neurotransmitter release → auditory nerve activation
#### Ionic Environment
- <font color="#245bdb">Endolymph: +80 mV, high K⁺</font>
- <font color="#245bdb">Perilymph: -50 mV</font>

# 3. Auditory Nerve Fibres (Spiral Ganglion  Cells)

## 3.1 Coding Dimensions 
Neurons encode:
1. **Frequency**
2. **Timing (phase locking)**
3. **Intensity**
## 3.2 Frequency Tuning

| ![[Pasted image 20260412065313.png\|300]] | ![[Pasted image 20260412065418.png\|225]] |
| ----------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
### Key Concepts
- **Frequency tuning curve**: at each frequency, what is the minimum sound intensity that will produce a response
- **Characteristic frequency (CF)**: most sensitive frequency
### Metrics
- **Threshold** → sensitivity
	- Minimum sound intensity at any frequency that produces a response (lower threshold = more sensitive)
- **Bandwidth** → selectivity
	- At a particular intensity, how wide is the band of frequencies that produces a response? (narrower = more frequency selective)
## 3.3 Temporal Coding 
- **Phase locking**:
    - AP only produced at a particular phase of sound wave
	    - Track wave form → Important in e.g. pitch perception
    - Declines with sound frequencies. Can only do strong at low frequencies (**< 3 kHz**)

# 4. Central Auditory Pathway

## 4.1 Pathway Overview (ascending)
Cochlea → CN (DCN/VCN) → SOC → IC → MGB → Auditory Cortex

## 4.2 Cochlear Nucleus (CN) 
### Key Features
- First processing station
- First **inhibitory synapses**
- First **convergence/divergence**
### Subdivisions (Division of Labours)
- DCN, anterior VCN, posterior VCN contain distinct cell types, so they are anatomically and physiologically speciallised
![[Pasted image 20260806154041.png|475]]
1. **Ventral CN**
    - Fast, precise, simple responses
    - → Superior Olive
2. **Dorsal CN**
    - Complex processing
    - → Inferior Colliculus (higher of auditory system)
### Tonotopic Map of Frequency
- Neurons in CN projects by frequency
![[Pasted image 20260412073604.png|375]]

## 4.3 Superior Olivary Complex (SOC) 
### Function
- First site of **convergence of input from 2 ears** → **Sound localisation**
- Send projections back to chochlea
### Cues for Sound Localisation
1. **ITD (Interaural Time Difference)**
    - Most useful at low frequency
2. **ILD (Interaural Level Difference)**
    - Most useful at high frequency
### Subnuclei
- **MSO** (medial superior olive) → ITD detection
	- Complex mechanisms. Different in birds and mammals
- **LSO** (lateral superior olive)→ ILD detection
    - Mechanism: ipsilateral excitation + contralateral inhibition

## 4.4 Inferior Colliculus (IC) 

| ![[Pasted image 20260412080255.png\|350]] | ![[Pasted image 20260412080318.png\|475]] |
| ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
### Location
- The central nucleus of IC is surrounded by dorsal cortex and lateral nucleus
### Role
- **Major integration centre**
  Site of convergence from ascending and descending pathways
- Obligatory relay in ascending pathway
### Functions
- Integrates:
    - MSO (low-freq ITD)
    - LSO (high-freq ILD)
    - Multisensory inputs
### Project to SC - Visual System
![[Pasted image 20260412080727.png|475]]
- IC has projections to SC. Sound info in SC is mapped by spatial origin → May be a mechanism for auditory system to communicate with eye motion / visual system

## 4.5 Medial Geniculate Body (= Auditory Thalamus) 
### Subdivisions
1. **Ventral MGN**
	- **Primary auditory pathway** (relatively simple responses)
    - Info relay: receives auditory info from IC, and projects it to A1
2. **Medial MGN**
    - Involved in attention, arousal, multisensory integration
3. **Dorsal MGN**
    - Functions affected by prior experience (plasticity, learning)
    - Involved in higher-order auditory processing

## 4.6 Auditory Cortex 

### Location
- A1: Heschl’s gyrus 
### Organisation
- Complex tonotopic maps 
	- Multiple frequency representations in different areas - Tonotopic mapping disappears in high-level VF

# 5. Cortical Organisation Pinciples

## 5.1 Hierarchical Structure (不考)
### 3 Levels of auditory cortex (A1)
1. **Core**
    - Primary input
    - Tonotopic
2. **Belt**
    - Secondary processing
    - Non-tonotopic
3. **Parabelt**
    - Higher-order processing
    - No direct thalamic input
## 5.2 Functional Hierarchy 
- **Simple sounds** → core
- **Complex sounds (pitch, motion, sequences)** → progressively more distant belt/parabelt

## 5.3 Hemispheric Specialisation 
- **Right hemisphere** → pitch direction
- **Left hemisphere** → temporal processing (sound duration)

## 5.4 Spatial Representation 
- No precise auditory spatial map in A1
- Neurons are broadly tuned to sound location and typically respond to contralateral space

## 5.5 Dorsal/Ventral Streams
![[Pasted image 20260412083842.png|475]]
### Classical Model
- **Dorsal (“where”)**
- **Ventral (“what”)**
### Updated View
- More accurate:
    - **Dorsal: Auditory–motor pathway**
    - **Ventral: Auditory–memory pathway**
    
## 5.6 Integration Principle 
- Real-world hearing (e.g. speech):  
    → requires multiple cortical areas working together

# Example Question


## Overview

The auditory system is a hierarchical and parallel processing system that converts sound waves into neural signals and interprets them as meaningful auditory percepts. Its organisation extends from the peripheral auditory apparatus to central cortical regions and is characterised by precise frequency mapping, bilateral integration, and extensive feedback modulation .

Auditory processing begins in the **peripheral auditory system**, which consists of the outer, middle, and inner ear. The **outer ear**, including the <font color="#c00000">pinna, concha, and external auditory canal</font>, collects sound waves and funnels them toward the <font color="#c00000">tympanic membrane</font>. It serves functions of <font color="#c00000">sound amplification</font> and <font color="#c00000">localisation through direction-dependent filtering</font>. The **middle ear** contains the <font color="#c00000">ossicles (malleus, incus, and stapes)</font> and the <font color="#c00000">Eustachian tube</font>. Its primary role is to <font color="#c00000">overcome impedance mismatch</font> between <font color="#c00000">air and cochlear fluid</font>. Without this mechanism, approximately 99.9% of sound energy would be reflected. This problem is solved by <font color="#c00000">pressure amplification</font> through<font color="#c00000"> lever action of the ossicles</font> and the <font color="#c00000">difference in area between the large tympanic membrane and the small stapes footplate</font>, thereby increasing pressure transmission to the cochlea. Additionally, the <font color="#c00000">stapedius reflex</font> reduces transmission in response to loud sounds, protecting the auditory system .

The **inner ear**, specifically the cochlea, is responsible for transduction. The <font color="#c00000">basilar membrane</font> exhibits tonotopic organisation, with the stiff, narrow base responding to high frequencies and the flexible, wide apex responding to low frequencies. This relationship reflects underlying physical properties, where increased stiffness corresponds to higher frequencies . Within the cochlea, **inner hair cells (IHCs)** provide the <font color="#c00000">primary sensory output</font>, while **outer hair cells (OHCs)** serve to <font color="#c00000">amplify mechanical responses</font>. Sound-induced movement of the basilar membrane <font color="#c00000">deflects stereocilia</font>, <font color="#c00000">altering tension in tip links and opening mechanically gated potassium channels</font>. Potassium influx depolarises the cell, leading to calcium entry and neurotransmitter release onto auditory nerve fibres. This process is facilitated by the unique ionic environment, with potassium-rich endolymph (+80 mV) and relatively negative perilymph (-50 mV) .

Auditory nerve fibres, arising from <font color="#c00000">spiral ganglion cells</font>, encode sound using three key dimensions: <font color="#c00000">frequency, intensity, and timing</font>. Frequency tuning is described by tuning curves, with each neuron having a <font color="#c00000">characteristic frequency</font> at which it is most sensitive. Sensitivity is reflected by threshold, and selectivity by bandwidth. <font color="#c00000">Temporal coding occurs through phase locking</font>, in which action potentials are generated at specific phases of the sound wave, particularly for low-frequency sounds .

Central processing occurs along the **ascending auditory pathway**, which follows a well-defined sequence: <font color="#c00000">cochlea → cochlear nuclei → superior olivary complex → lateral lemniscus → inferior colliculus → medial geniculate body → auditory cortex.</font> The **cochlear nuclei** represent the first central processing stage and exhibit functional specialisation. The <font color="#c00000">ventral cochlear nucleus</font> produces <font color="#c00000">fast and precise responses</font> important for<font color="#c00000"> temporal coding</font>, while the <font color="#c00000">dorsal cochlear nucleus</font> performs more <font color="#c00000">complex processing</font>.

The **superior olivary complex (SOC)** is the first site of<font color="#c00000"> binaural convergence </font>and is essential for<font color="#c00000"> sound localisation</font>. It computes <font color="#c00000">interaural time differences (ITDs)</font>, primarily in the <font color="#c00000">medial superior olive</font> for <font color="#c00000">low-frequency sounds</font>, and <font color="#c00000">interaural level differences (ILDs)</font>, primarily in the <font color="#c00000">lateral superior olive</font> for <font color="#c00000">high-frequency sounds</font>. These computations rely on mechanisms such as coincidence detection and interactions between excitatory and inhibitory inputs .

The **inferior colliculus (IC)** in the midbrain acts as a major integration centre and obligatory relay, combining inputs from ascending pathways and descending cortical projections. It <font color="#c00000">integrates spatial cues, including ITD and ILD, as well as multisensory information</font>, and projects to the superior colliculus, linking auditory processing with orienting responses . From the IC, signals are transmitted to the **medial geniculate body (MGB)** of the thalamus. The ventral MGB functions as the primary <font color="#c00000">relay to cortex</font>, the medial MGB is involved in attention and<font color="#c00000"> multisensory integration</font>, and the dorsal MGB contributes to<font color="#c00000"> learning and plasticity</font>.

Auditory information then reaches the **auditory cortex**, located in Heschl’s gyrus. The cortex exhibits complex <font color="#c00000">tonotopic organisation</font> with multiple frequency maps. Hemispheric specialisation is evident, with the right hemisphere preferentially processing pitch and the left processing temporal aspects such as sound duration.

At higher levels, auditory processing diverges into functional streams. The classical division into dorsal (“where”) and ventral (“what”) streams has been refined into a **dorsal auditory–motor pathway** and a **ventral auditory–memory pathway**, reflecting their roles in <font color="#c00000">action guidance</font> and<font color="#c00000"> sound recognition</font> respectively. Real-world auditory perception, such as speech, requires integration across multiple cortical areas.

Finally, the auditory system includes **descending (feedback) pathways**, which play a crucial role in adaptive processing. Cortical projections to the<font color="#c00000"> thalamus</font> and <font color="#c00000">inferior colliculus</font> modulate sensory processing and support learning and plasticity. The **olivocochlear system**, projecting from the <font color="#c00000">superior olive to the cochlea</font>, modulates <font color="#c00000">auditory nerve sensitivity</font> and<font color="#c00000"> cochlear amplification</font> through lateral and medial components . Clinically, cochlear implants exploit these principles by bypassing damaged hair cells and directly stimulating auditory nerve fibres.

In summary, the auditory system is a highly organised network that transforms sound into neural representations through precise mechanical, neural, and cortical processing, enabling perception, localisation, and adaptive behaviour.
