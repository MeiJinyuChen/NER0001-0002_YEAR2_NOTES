# 1. overall Organization of motor and somatosensory pathways

## 1.1 CNS structure

- **Forebrain → Brainstem → Spinal cord**
- Pathways organized into:
    - **Descending (motor) tracts**
    - **Ascending (sensory) tracts**
- Tracts: A collection of axons located inside the CNS.
- Nerves: A collection of axons located outside the CNS, in the Peripheral Nervous System (PNS).

## 1.2 Body map

The somatopic body map applies to both the motor and somatosensory cortices.
- It is not the case that each corticospinal neuron controls one muscle—— they have widespread terminals, eg there is no corticospinal neuron specific for biceps brachii
- No body map for visceral motor / visceral pain

# 3. motor systems (descending)

## 3.1 Motor neuron hierarchy

### Upper Motor Neurons (UMN)

- Project from **cortex** to ventral horn
- Project via descending tracts

### Lower Motor Neurons (LMN)

- Location: **ventral horn (lamina IX)**
- Directly innervate muscle

## 3.2 Motor unit

- **1 α-motor neuron + all extrafusal muscle fibres it innervates**
- Force controlled by:
    - **Motor unit recruitment**

## 3.3 Muscle fibre types

- **Extrafusal fibres** → generate force
- **Intrafusal fibres (muscle spindle)** → sensory (stretch)
- Innervation:
    - **α motor neurons → extrafusal**
    - **γ motor neurons → intrafusal**
- Motor units: Each α motor neuron and the extrafusal muscle fibres it innervates constitute a motor unit. The number of motor unit recruited helps to determine the strength of muscle contraction.

## 3.4 Descending Pathways

1. **Corticospinal**
2. **(Cortico-)rubrospinal**
3. **Reticulospinal**
4. **Vestibulospinal**
- Rubrospinal and reticulospinal pathways arise in the cortex but involve relays in the brainstem.

## 4.7 Synaptic organization

- CST axons:
    - Mostly synapse on **interneurons**
    - Some directly on motor neurons (especially hand)
- Effects:
    - Excitatory stimulation (direct or via excitatory interneurons)
    - Inhibitory control (via inhibitory interneurons)
- More CST axons end on inhibitory interneurons than excitatory. If the descending tracts are injured, there is more excitation of motor neurons producing enhanced reflexes (hyperreflexia).

# 5. clinical lesions in CST

## 5.1 Lower motor neuron lesion (LMN)

### Cause 

peripheral nerve damage
- Death of the cell body → axon degenerates (e.g. poliomyelitis)
- Axons being injured

### Signs

- <span style="background:#fff88f">Flaccid paralysis</span>
	 = Muscles lose the ability to contract.
- <span style="background:#fff88f">Rapid muscle wasting</span>
- <span style="background:#fff88f">Fibrillation & Fasciculation</span>
	 = Random contraction of either individual muscle fibres or a small group of muscle fibres

## 5.2 Upper motor neuron lesion (UMN)

### Cause

- Stroke (lack of blood supply → death of cell bodies / axons being injured)
- Spinal cord injury

### Result

- When axons die in the CNS (e.g. lateral CST), they get replaced by astrocytes.

### Signs

- <span style="background:#fff88f">Spastic paralysis</span>
	 = Increased, involuntary, velocity-dependent muscle tone that cause resistance to movement
- <span style="background:#fff88f">Very little muscle wasting</span>
- Babinski sign (= extensor plantar reflex)
	 = An abnormal reflex where the big toe extends upward and the other toes fan out in response to stroking the sole of the foot
	- Until the corticospinal tracts are myelinated, babies also show a positive Babinski sign
	- Can be seen in people after a seizure. The motor cortex has been overwhelmed by disorganized eletctrical activitym, so that the CST has temporarily lost its inhibitory control over the primitive Babinski reflex

## 5.3 Locked-in syndrome

### Cause

**basilar artery supplying the CST is blocked (pons)**

### Signs

- Conscious but cannot move or speak

## 5.4 Lesions to pyramids

### Signs

- Primates can still move (supported by other tracts) but have inaccurate hand movements.
	- Indicates that other descending tracts can perform most of the voluntary movements. But CST is particularly important for hand movements.

## 5.5 Lesions in motor cortex

- Lesions of motor cortex have little effect in non-primates but large effects in primates, because of the demand in using hands
- Loss of motor cortex has more effect than loss of the CST because of reticulospinal and rubrospinal pathways

### For non-primates

- Perfectly fine for removing motor and sensory cortices

### For primates

#### For kids

e.g. Hemispherectomy for intractable epilepsy
- Almost no effect because of plasticity

#### For adults

- Paralysis on contraletral body side

# Exam Question

## 1. Give a brief account of the anatomy and functions of the corticospinal tract.

The corticospinal tract (CST) is the principal descending motor pathway responsible for voluntary movement, particularly skilled distal limb and hand control. It is especially developed in primates, where precise motor function is essential. The CST forms a major component of the pyramidal system and links cerebral cortex motor regions to spinal motor circuits.

Corticospinal neurons originate primarily in the primary motor cortex (M1), but also arise from premotor cortex, supplementary motor area (SMA), and parietal cortex. Premotor cortex is particularly involved in externally guided movement planning, while SMA contributes to internally generated movement sequences. Parietal contributions mainly modulate sensory processing relevant to movement.

Axons descend through the corona radiata and posterior limb of the internal capsule, a critical white matter bottleneck. From there, fibres pass through the crus cerebri of the midbrain, the ventral pons, and then form the medullary pyramids. At the caudal medulla, approximately 85% of fibres decussate in the pyramidal decussation, crossing the midline to form the lateral corticospinal tract. This crossed pathway descends in the lateral spinal cord and controls distal limb musculature, particularly fine finger movements. The remaining ~15% continue ipsilaterally as the ventral corticospinal tract, primarily influencing axial and trunk muscles involved in posture.

Within the spinal cord, CST fibres terminate mainly on interneurons, though some make direct monosynaptic connections with α-motor neurons, especially those controlling the hand. This direct connectivity underlies dexterous movement. CST influences both excitatory and inhibitory circuits, with substantial inhibitory control suppressing primitive spinal reflexes.

Functionally, the CST is central to voluntary movement execution, motor precision, and fractionated muscle control. It does not simply activate muscles individually, but rather coordinates complex movement patterns through distributed spinal networks.

Clinical lesions demonstrate CST importance. Upper motor neuron lesions, such as stroke or spinal cord injury, produce contralateral spastic paralysis, hyperreflexia, and Babinski sign due to loss of inhibitory descending input. Lower motor neurons remain intact, so muscle wasting is limited. In contrast, isolated CST damage in primates disproportionately impairs hand dexterity while sparing gross movement due to compensation by reticulospinal and rubrospinal systems.

Thus, the corticospinal tract represents a highly specialized pathway integrating cortical planning with spinal motor execution, essential for refined voluntary motor control.

## 2. Compare upper and lower motor neuron lesions, including anatomical basis and clinical signs.

Motor deficits can arise from lesions affecting either upper motor neurons (UMNs) or lower motor neurons (LMNs), producing distinct clinical syndromes based on anatomical location and functional disruption.

Upper motor neurons originate in the cerebral cortex and brainstem and descend to influence spinal motor circuits, particularly via the corticospinal tract. Lower motor neurons are α-motor neurons located in the ventral horn of the spinal cord or cranial motor nuclei, directly innervating skeletal muscle.

LMN lesions involve damage to the final common pathway between CNS and muscle, including ventral roots, peripheral nerves, or anterior horn cells. Causes include poliomyelitis, peripheral neuropathy, or trauma. Because direct muscle innervation is lost, muscles cannot contract, producing flaccid paralysis. Muscle tone is reduced, reflexes are diminished or absent, and rapid denervation atrophy occurs. Fasciculations and fibrillations are characteristic due to spontaneous instability of denervated motor units.

In contrast, UMN lesions affect descending control systems such as the CST while sparing LMNs. Common causes include stroke, spinal cord injury, or multiple sclerosis. Since inhibitory cortical input is disrupted, spinal reflex circuits become disinhibited. This produces spastic paralysis, characterized by increased muscle tone, hyperreflexia, velocity-dependent resistance to movement, and exaggerated tendon reflexes. Babinski sign, where the great toe dorsiflexes upon plantar stimulation, reflects release of primitive reflex pathways normally suppressed by CST activity. Muscle wasting is minimal because peripheral innervation remains intact.

Anatomically, the distinction reflects differing roles: LMNs generate force directly, while UMNs modulate and coordinate movement. More CST fibres terminate on inhibitory interneurons than excitatory circuits; therefore UMN damage disproportionately enhances reflex activity.

Special lesion syndromes further illustrate this hierarchy. Locked-in syndrome, caused by pontine damage, interrupts descending CST pathways bilaterally, producing quadriplegia despite preserved consciousness. Decerebrate rigidity results from lesions above vestibular nuclei, causing unchecked extensor output.

Understanding UMN versus LMN syndromes is clinically crucial for lesion localization. UMN lesions suggest central pathology, while LMN signs indicate peripheral or spinal motor neuron damage. Together, these syndromes reveal the hierarchical organization of motor control from cortex to muscle.

## 3. Describe the major descending motor pathways other than the corticospinal tract and their functions.

Although the corticospinal tract is the primary pathway for fine voluntary movement, several additional descending motor systems are essential for posture, balance, gross movement, and autonomic motor integration. These include rubrospinal, reticulospinal, and vestibulospinal pathways.

The rubrospinal tract originates in the red nucleus of the midbrain, which receives cortical input. Fibres decussate shortly after origin and descend primarily to cervical spinal levels. In humans, this system is relatively minor compared with other mammals, but it contributes to <font color="#c00000">distal limb movement and may partially compensate following corticospinal injury</font>.

The reticulospinal pathways arise from pontine and medullary reticular formation. These tracts receive widespread input from cerebral cortex, sensory systems, and autonomic centers. The medial (pontine) reticulospinal tract<font color="#c00000"> facilitates extensor tone and posture</font>, while the lateral (medullary) reticulospinal tract modulates movement and <font color="#c00000">inhibits extensor reflexes</font>. Functionally, reticulospinal systems <font color="#c00000">regulate posture, locomotion, trunk control, and gross motor coordination</font>. They are also crucial for<font color="#c00000"> arousal </font>and integrate respiratory and autonomic functions. Additionally, reticular nuclei contribute to descending pain modulation via raphe pathways.

Vestibulospinal pathways originate from <font color="#c00000">vestibular nuclei</font>, particularly the lateral vestibular nucleus. These tracts <font color="#c00000">descend ipsilaterally without decussation</font> and primarily facilitate extensor muscles to <font color="#c00000">maintain balance and antigravity posture</font>. They are critical for equilibrium and reflexive <font color="#c00000">postural adjustments</font> but do <font color="#c00000">not significantly contribute to voluntary movement</font>.

Collectively, these non-corticospinal pathways are often termed ventromedial descending systems. They emphasize whole-body posture and coordinated movement rather than fine dexterity. Their functional importance is highlighted by lesion studies: animals with corticospinal damage may retain substantial gross movement due to reticulospinal compensation, whereas damage to vestibular systems can produce severe postural deficits such as decerebrate rigidity.

Thus, descending motor control is distributed across multiple parallel systems. While the CST provides precision, rubrospinal, reticulospinal, and vestibulospinal pathways ensure posture, stability, and integrated motor behavior. This distributed architecture increases redundancy and permits complex movement across diverse behavioural contexts.

## 4. Overview

The motor pathway is a hierarchical system that transmits signals from the cerebral cortex to skeletal muscles, enabling voluntary movement, posture, and reflex control. It is organised within the central nervous system along a descending axis from **forebrain to brainstem to spinal cord**, and operates through **descending motor tracts**, which function in parallel with ascending sensory pathways. A key organisational principle is the **somatotopic body map**, which is present in both motor and somatosensory cortices, although this mapping does not apply to visceral motor systems .

At the level of the **spinal cord**, organisation is based on segmental anatomy and functional zones. Spinal nerves attach via **dorsal roots (sensory input)** and **ventral roots (motor output)**, with each cord segment corresponding to a specific body region. The grey matter is divided into **Rexed laminae I–X**, with the **dorsal horn (laminae I–VI)** processing sensory input and the **ventral horn (lamina IX)** containing motor neurons. Notably, laminae I–II (substantia gelatinosa) process nociceptive information, while lamina IX contains **α motor neurons**, which directly innervate skeletal muscle. These motor neurons are organised somatotopically, with **lateral neurons controlling distal muscles (e.g., hands)** and **medial neurons controlling proximal muscles (e.g., neck)** .

Motor control operates through a hierarchy of **upper motor neurons (UMNs)** and **lower motor neurons (LMNs)**. UMNs originate in the cortex or brainstem and project to LMNs, which reside in the ventral horn and directly innervate muscles. The fundamental functional unit is the **motor unit**, defined as a single α motor neuron and all the extrafusal muscle fibres it innervates. Muscle force is regulated by **motor unit recruitment**, while **γ motor neurons** innervate intrafusal fibres within muscle spindles, providing sensory feedback about muscle stretch .

The principal descending pathway for voluntary movement is the **corticospinal tract (CST)**. This tract originates from multiple cortical areas, including the **primary motor cortex**, **premotor cortex**, **supplementary motor area**, and **parietal cortex**. These areas contribute different functional roles: the premotor cortex mediates externally guided movements, the supplementary motor area supports internally generated actions, and the parietal cortex modulates sensory input to the spinal cord .

CST axons descend through a well-defined pathway: **cortex → corona radiata → posterior limb of the internal capsule → cerebral peduncle (midbrain) → pons → pyramids of the medulla → decussation → spinal cord**. At the caudal medulla, approximately **85% of fibres cross the midline**, forming the **lateral corticospinal tract**, which controls distal limb movements. The remaining **15% form the ventral corticospinal tract**, which primarily controls axial and trunk muscles . This crossing establishes the principle of **contralateral motor control**.

Within the spinal cord, CST axons synapse predominantly on **interneurons**, although some make direct connections with motor neurons, particularly those controlling fine hand movements. These synaptic effects can be both **excitatory and inhibitory**, reflecting the integration of motor commands. Importantly, more CST axons synapse onto inhibitory interneurons, which explains why damage to descending pathways leads to increased reflex activity due to loss of inhibition .

In addition to the corticospinal tract, several other descending pathways contribute to motor control. The **cortico-rubrospinal pathway** involves relays in the red nucleus and contributes to limb movement control. The **reticulospinal pathways**, originating from the reticular formation in the pons and medulla, regulate posture, gross movements, and arousal, integrating inputs from the cortex and sensory systems. They also contribute to autonomic functions such as respiratory control. The **vestibulospinal pathway**, originating from the lateral vestibular nucleus, controls balance and posture by inhibiting unwanted movements and maintaining extensor tone; it does not decussate and is not involved in voluntary movement .

Functionally, the motor pathway enables precise voluntary movement, posture maintenance, and coordination between sensory input and motor output. The CST is particularly important for fine motor control, especially of the hands, while other descending pathways can compensate for broader aspects of movement.

Clinically, lesions in the motor pathway produce distinct patterns. **Lower motor neuron lesions**, typically due to peripheral nerve damage, result in flaccid paralysis, muscle wasting, and fasciculations. In contrast, **upper motor neuron lesions**, caused by stroke or spinal cord injury, lead to spastic paralysis, hyperreflexia, and increased muscle tone due to loss of inhibitory control . A notable condition is **locked-in syndrome**, caused by lesions in the pons, in which patients are conscious but unable to move or speak. Damage to the pyramidal tracts can produce the **Babinski sign**, reflecting loss of corticospinal inhibition.

In summary, the motor pathway is a highly organised hierarchical system that integrates cortical planning, brainstem modulation, and spinal execution to produce coordinated movement and maintain motor control.
