# 2. Fibre Pathways

## 2.1 Three types of fibres

### A. Association fibres

- Connect **within same hemisphere**
- Types:
    - Short (intralobar)
    - Long (interlobar)

#### Key tracts

- **Superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF)**  
    → frontal ↔ parietal/occipital  
    → speech (L), visuospatial processing (R)
- **Arcuate fasciculus**  
    → frontal ↔ occipitotemporal  
    → language, social communication, working memory, complex limb motor coordination
- **Uncinate fasciculus**  
    → orbitofrontal ↔ anterior temporal  
    → naming, empathy
- **Inferior longitudinal fasciculus**  
    → occipital ↔ temporal  
    → **object & face recognition**
	→ Important in **ventral visual stream** functions
- **Cingulum**  
    → frontal/parietal ↔ parahippocampal and other temporal areas (limbic)

### B. Commissural fibres

- Connect **between hemispheres**

<img src="assets/Pasted%20image%2020260410041525.png" alt="Pasted image 20260410041525">

#### Main structure: **Corpus callosum**

- Contain almost all commissural fibres
- Parts:
    - Genu (anterior)
    - Body
    - Splenium (posterior)
- Callosal fibres linking frontal and occipital poles:
	- Frontal forceps = forceps minor
	- Occipital forceps = forceps major

#### Exception

- Commissural fibres between **temporal lobes** →  Motsly part of the **anterior commissure**
- Some areas **NOT directly connected** via commissural fibres (but only indirectly via connections through association cortical areas):
    - **Hand somatosensory cortex**
    - Large parts of **primary visual cortex**

#### Other commissures

- **Anterior commissure**  
    → middle and inferior temporal gyri + temporal olfactory areas + olfactory bulbs
- **Posterior commissure**  
	→ Dosal aspect of the rostral end of the cerebral aqueduct
    → Important in **bilateral pupillary light reflex** (parasympathetic component of CN III)
- **Habenular commissure**  
	→ In front of pineal gland
    → connects habenular nuclei

#### Clinical 

##### Split-brain (corpus callosotomy)

- Used for resistant epilepsy
- Isolate affected hemisphere by cutting corpus callosum
- Most of healthy functionings are reserved

##### Hemispherectomy

- Used for intractable epilepsy
- Isolate affected hemisphere by:
	1. Removing it entirely + all subcortical structures (anatomical hemispherectomy)
	2. Severing all connections to functional hemisphere (functional hemispherectomy) → Partial anatomic resection
- Severed deficits

##### Agenesis of corpus callosum

- The most common human brain malformation
- Symptoms:
    - Cognitive deficits
    - Visual problems
    - Seizures
    - Social/behavioral disorders

### C. Projection fibres

- Connect cortex ↔ subcortical structures
	- e.g. compus straitum, thalamus, brain stem, spinal cord

#### Organization

- **Corona radiata** → diffuse fibres
- **Internal capsule** → compact bundle

##### Internal capsule components

- **Anterior limb**  
    → mediodorsal nucleus of thalamus ↔ frontal cortex
- **Genu + posterior limb**  
    → corticospinal/corticobulbar motor fibres + thalamocortical connections
- **Retrolenticular**  
    → visual & auditory pathways (medial/lateral geniculate thalamic bodies ↔ **visual & auditory cortices**)

# 3. Cortex

## 3.1 Neocortex

- 6 layers

| Layer | Function                         |
| ----- | -------------------------------- |
| 2, 3  | Often cortical association areas |
| 4     | Input from thalamus              |
| 5     | Key (extracortical) output layer |
| 6     | Feedback output to thalamus      |

### Brodmann Areas

- Based on **cytoarchitecture**

## 3.2 Non-standard cortex

### A. Isocortex

= Typical cortex with 6 layers
- **Neocortex**

### B. Allocortex 

= primitive cortex with fewer layers (usually 3 layers)
- **Hippocampus**: One cell layers surrounded by 2 white layers on each side

### C. Periallocortex 

= transitional cortex between allocortex and neocortex
- **Entorhinal cortex** 

### Transition sequence

- <font color="#245bdb">Allocortex → Periallocortex → Proisocortex → Isocortex</font>

## 3.3 Special neurons 

- **Von Economo neurons (VENs)**
    - Speciliased pyrmidal neurons with specific morphological characteristics 
    - In *Homo sapiens*
    - Located in layer V of:
        - ACC (anterior cingulate cortex)
        - Fronto-insular cortex
    - Function:
        - Regulation of complex social behaviour

# Example Questions

## 1. “Give a brief account of the organisation of the cerebral cortex, including its anatomical structure and major functional divisions.”

The cerebral cortex is the outermost layer of the brain and represents the highest level of neural processing in humans. It is responsible for integrating sensory information, generating motor commands, and supporting higher cognitive functions such as language, memory, and decision-making. Its organisation reflects both anatomical structure and functional specialisation, allowing efficient and distributed processing of information.

Anatomically, the cortex is divided into four major lobes: frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital. These lobes are defined by key sulci, which serve as important landmarks. The central sulcus separates the frontal and parietal lobes, the lateral sulcus divides the temporal lobe from the frontal and parietal lobes, and the parieto-occipital sulcus helps define the boundary of the occipital lobe. The cortex is highly folded into gyri and sulci, a process known as gyrification, which increases surface area and allows more neurons to be packed within the limited volume of the skull.

At the microscopic level, the neocortex is organised into six distinct layers, each with specific input-output characteristics. Layer IV primarily receives sensory input from the thalamus, making it prominent in primary sensory areas. Layer V serves as a major output layer, sending projections to subcortical structures such as the spinal cord. Layer VI provides feedback to the thalamus, forming reciprocal loops that regulate information flow. This layered organisation reflects a hierarchical processing system in which information is received, processed, and transmitted to other brain regions.

The cortex is also connected through three major types of fibre systems. Association fibres connect regions within the same hemisphere and support integration of information across different cortical areas. Commissural fibres, primarily via the corpus callosum, connect the two hemispheres and enable interhemispheric communication. Projection fibres connect the cortex with subcortical structures, such as the thalamus and brainstem, and are essential for sensory input and motor output.

Functionally, the cortex is organised into specialised regions and pathways. One of the most important examples is the division of visual processing into dorsal and ventral streams. The dorsal stream projects from the occipital lobe to the parietal cortex and is involved in spatial processing and action guidance, often referred to as the “where” or “how” pathway. In contrast, the ventral stream projects to the temporal cortex and is responsible for object recognition and identification, known as the “what” pathway.

The frontal cortex plays a key role in motor control and executive function. The primary motor cortex is responsible for voluntary movement and is organised somatotopically. The premotor cortex and supplementary motor area are involved in planning and coordinating movements. More anteriorly, the prefrontal cortex supports higher cognitive processes such as working memory, decision-making, and behavioural regulation.

The parietal cortex integrates sensory information and contributes to spatial awareness and attention. Damage to this region, particularly in the right hemisphere, can result in spatial neglect. The temporal cortex is involved in object recognition and memory, with specialised regions such as the fusiform face area supporting face recognition.

In summary, the cerebral cortex is organised into anatomically distinct lobes, layered microstructures, and interconnected networks that support a wide range of functions. Its hierarchical and distributed organisation enables complex behaviours and cognition, making it a central component of human brain function.
