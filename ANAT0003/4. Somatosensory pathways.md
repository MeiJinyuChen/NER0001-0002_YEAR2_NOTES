## 1.1 Shingles (herpes zoster)
### Cause and signs
The **reactivation** of varicella zoster virus (VZV)
1. Initially causes chickenpox
2. VZV be latent in primary sensory neuron cell bodies (in sensory ganglia e.g. DRG)
3. **Reactivation**: 
   Later in life (e.g. ageing, immunosuppression), VZV:
	  - Becomes active again inside DRG neurons
	   - Travels down sensory neurons to skin
	   - **Cause a localized skin rash in the corresponding dermatome -- band-like pattern**
	     (Each DRG corresponds to one dermatome)
4. Post-herpetic neuralgia
   - Persistent  burning pain after rash resolves, cannot be reduced by analgesics
   - Due to:
     1. Damage to sensory neurons
     2. Central sensitization

# Example Question


## 1. Describe the anatomical pathways by which tactile and nociceptive information from the body reach the cerebral cortex, highlighting key differences.

1. VERSION 1
Somatosensory information from the body is transmitted to the cerebral cortex via two principal ascending pathways: the dorsal column-medial lemniscus (DCML) system and the spinothalamic tract. These systems differ in sensory modality, anatomical course, and decussation site.

The DCML system conveys discriminative touch, vibration, and conscious proprioception. Primary sensory neurons with cell bodies in dorsal root ganglia (DRG) enter the spinal cord and ascend ipsilaterally within the dorsal columns. Fibres from the lower body travel in the fasciculus gracilis, while upper body fibres travel in the fasciculus cuneatus. These fibres synapse in the nucleus gracilis or cuneatus in the medulla. Second-order neurons then decussate as<font color="#c00000"> internal arcuate fibres </font>and <font color="#c00000">ascend contralaterally as the medial lemniscus</font> to the <font color="#c00000">ventral posterior lateral (VPL) nucleus of the thalamus</font>. Third-order neurons project to the primary somatosensory cortex.

In contrast, the spinothalamic tract transmits pain, temperature, and crude touch. Primary afferents enter the dorsal horn and synapse in <font color="#c00000">superficial laminae</font>. Second-order neurons cross the midline within the spinal cord shortly after entry and ascend contralaterally in the anterolateral system to the thalamus. From the thalamus, projections reach the somatosensory cortex.

Thus, the DCML pathway crosses in the medulla and preserves highly precise sensory discrimination, whereas the spinothalamic tract crosses early in the spinal cord and primarily signals protective nociceptive information. Clinical lesions demonstrate this distinction: spinal cord damage may produce ipsilateral loss of fine touch but contralateral pain loss below the lesion.

2. VERSION 2
The somatosensory system conveys sensory information from the body to the cerebral cortex primarily through two major ascending pathways: the dorsal column-medial lemniscus (DCML) system and the spinothalamic tract. These pathways differ significantly in modality, anatomy, and clinical significance.

The DCML system transmits <font color="#c00000">discriminative touch, vibration, and conscious proprioception</font>. Primary sensory neurons, whose cell bodies are located in dorsal root ganglia (DRG), receive information from <font color="#c00000">mechanoreceptors and proprioceptors</font>. Their heavily myelinated Aβ and Aα fibres enter the spinal cord and ascend <font color="#c00000">ipsilaterally </font>within the<font color="#c00000"> dorsal columns</font>. Lower body information travels via the <font color="#c00000">fasciculus gracilis</font>, while upper body input ascends through the <font color="#c00000">fasciculus cuneatus</font>. These fibres synapse in the <font color="#c00000">nucleus gracilis and nucleus cuneatus</font> of the<font color="#c00000"> medulla</font>. Second-order neurons then <font color="#c00000">decussate</font> as<font color="#c00000"> internal arcuate fibres</font> and ascend <font color="#c00000">contralaterally</font> as the <font color="#c00000">medial lemniscus</font> to the <font color="#c00000">ventral posterior lateral (VPL) nucleus</font> of the thalamus. Third-order neurons project to the <font color="#c00000">primary somatosensory cortex</font>. This pathway allows precise localization and high-fidelity sensory discrimination.

In contrast, the spinothalamic tract transmits <font color="#c00000">pain, temperature, and crude touch</font>. Small Aδ and C fibres enter the dorsal horn and synapse primarily in superficial laminae I, II, and V. Second-order neurons decussate within one or two spinal segments via the <font color="#c00000">anterior white commissure</font> and ascend contralaterally within the<font color="#c00000"> anterolateral system</font> to the thalamus. From there, third-order neurons project to the somatosensory cortex.<font color="#c00000"> Collaterals also project to the reticular formation and periaqueductal grey (PAG), integrating arousal and pain modulation.</font>

Thus, DCML fibres cross in the medulla, while spinothalamic fibres cross in the spinal cord. DCML provides rapid, precise sensory input, whereas spinothalamic pathways emphasize protective nociceptive signaling.

Clinically, spinal cord hemisection produces Brown-Séquard syndrome, with ipsilateral DCML deficits and contralateral pain and temperature loss. Understanding these anatomical distinctions is therefore critical for lesion localization and neurological diagnosis.


## 2. Briefly describe mechanisms of pain modulation in the CNS.

Pain perception is not purely passive but is actively modulated at multiple CNS levels through spinal gating and descending control systems.

At the spinal level, nociceptive signals from Aδ and C fibres synapse in superficial dorsal horn laminae. According to <font color="#c00000">gate control theory</font>, large-diameter Aβ mechanoreceptor fibres can suppress pain transmission by activating inhibitory interneurons in the dorsal horn. These<font color="#c00000"> interneurons inhibit nociceptive projection neurons</font>, reducing pain perception. This explains why rubbing injured tissue can lessen pain.

Descending pathways from higher brain centres also regulate nociception. The <font color="#c00000">cerebral cortex and hypothalamus</font> influence the<font color="#c00000"> periaqueductal grey (PAG)</font> in the <font color="#c00000">midbrain</font>, a major pain control centre. PAG neurons project to brainstem nuclei including the <font color="#c00000">raphe nuclei </font>and <font color="#c00000">locus coeruleus</font>, which send serotonergic (5-HT) and noradrenergic fibres to the<font color="#c00000"> superficial dorsal horn</font>. These neurotransmitters modulate spinal<font color="#c00000"> interneurons and projection neurons</font>, either suppressing or enhancing nociceptive transmission.

This system is clinically important because<font color="#c00000"> endogenous opioids, such as endorphins, act partly through PAG circuits, while opioid analgesics mimic this mechanism. </font>Chronic pain may result from maladaptive plasticity in dorsal horn neurons, microglial activation, and central sensitization, causing persistent pain even after tissue healing.

Therefore, pain modulation involves both peripheral sensory competition and powerful descending networks. These systems allow context-dependent regulation of pain but may also contribute to chronic pathological pain states.


## 3. Explain the functional significance of dermatomes and their clinical relevance.

Dermatomes are segmental areas of skin innervated by sensory fibres from individual spinal nerves or cranial nerves. Each dermatome corresponds to a specific dorsal root ganglion (DRG), allowing somatosensory input from defined body regions to enter the CNS in an organized segmental pattern.

Dermatomal organization is clinically valuable because sensory deficits or pain patterns can localize lesions to specific spinal nerve roots or ganglia. For example, disc herniation may compress individual nerve roots, producing predictable sensory symptoms in corresponding dermatomes.

A major clinical example is herpes zoster (shingles), caused by reactivation of latent varicella-zoster virus within DRG neurons. Following primary infection (chickenpox = varicella), the virus remains dormant in sensory ganglia. Reactivation later in life allows viral particles to travel along sensory nerves to the skin, producing a painful vesicular rash limited to the affected dermatome. Because each DRG supplies one dermatome, the rash often appears as a unilateral band-like distribution.

Post-herpetic neuralgia may persist after rash resolution due to peripheral sensory neuron damage and central sensitization within pain pathways, making it difficult to treat with standard analgesics.

Dermatomes also aid neurological examination, enabling clinicians to distinguish peripheral neuropathies, radiculopathies, or spinal cord lesions. Thus, dermatomal mapping provides a crucial anatomical framework linking peripheral sensory input to central nervous system organization and disease diagnosis.
