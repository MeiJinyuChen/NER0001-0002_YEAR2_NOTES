
### **I. The Physical Basis: Diffusion and Mobility**

#### **1. Diffusion and the Diffusion Potential**
- **Definition:** The movement of ions from an area of high concentration to low concentration.
- **Diffusion Potential:** A potential difference created when ions of opposite charges move at different speeds (mobilities) down a concentration gradient.
- **Determinants of Magnitude:**
    1. Concentration gradient.
    2. Differences in ion mobilities ($u$).
    3. Absolute Temperature ($T$).
- **Ion Mobility ($u$):** Depends on ion size and interactions with the solvent. Smaller ions generally have higher mobility ($D \propto 1/r$).

#### **2. Flux ($J$) and Fick’s First Law**
Flux is the amount of substance crossing a unit area per unit time.
- **Formula:** $J = -D \cdot \frac{dc}{dx}$
    - $D$: Diffusion coefficient.
    - $dc/dx$: Concentration gradient.
- **Net Flux:** In biological systems, net flux is the sum of the **chemical flux** (concentration) and the **electrical flux** (potential).
    
---

### **II. Thermodynamic Equilibrium: The Nernst Equation**

#### **1. Electrochemical Potential ($\mu$)**
The total energy of an ion in solution is the sum of its chemical and electrical potentials.
- **Formula:** $\mu = \mu_0 + RT \ln[C] + zFE$
    - $R$: Gas constant.
    - $T$: Absolute temperature.
    - $z$: Valence.
    - $F$: Faraday constant.
    - $E$: Electrical potential.
        
#### **2. The Nernst Equation**

At equilibrium, the net flux of an ion is zero ($J = 0$), meaning the chemical and electrical forces are equal and opposite.
- **General Formula:** $E_{ion} = \frac{RT}{zF} \ln \frac{[ion]_{out}}{[ion]_{in}}$
- **Simplified (at 20°C):** $E_{ion} = \frac{58}{z} \log_{10} \frac{[ion]_{out}}{[ion]_{in}}$
- **Simplified (at 37°C):** $E_{ion} = \frac{61.5}{z} \log_{10} \frac{[ion]_{out}}{[ion]_{in}}$
- **Exam Utility:** This calculates the "battery" voltage for any ion. If a cell is only permeable to $K^+$, the membrane potential ($V_m$) will equal $E_K$.
    
---

### **III. Steady-State and the GHK Equation**

In real neurons, the membrane is permeable to multiple ions simultaneously ($Na^+$, $K^+$, $Cl^-$). Because $V_m$ cannot be at the equilibrium potential for all ions at once, ions are in a "steady state," not true equilibrium.

#### **1. The Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz (GHK) Voltage Equation**
Calculates the resting membrane potential ($V_m$) based on the concentrations and relative permeabilities ($P$) of all permeant ions.
- **Formula:** $V_m = \frac{RT}{F} \ln \left( \frac{P_K [K^+]_o + P_{Na} [Na^+]_o + P_{Cl} [Cl^-]_i}{P_K [K^+]_i + P_{Na} [Na^+]_i + P_{Cl} [Cl^-]_o} \right)$
- **Key Insight:** $V_m$ is determined by the ion with the **highest relative permeability**. At rest, $P_K \gg P_{Na}$, so $V_m$ sits near $E_K$.
    
#### 2. Chord Conductance Equation
An alternative way to express $V_m$ using conductances ($g$) instead of permeabilities.
- **Formula:** $V_m = \frac{g_K E_K + g_{Na} E_{Na} + g_{Cl} E_{Cl}}{g_K + g_{Na} + g_{Cl}}$
    

---

### **IV. Maintenance of Gradients: The $Na^+/K^+$ Pump**
Because the membrane is slightly permeable to $Na^+$ at rest, $Na^+$ leaks in and $K^+$ leaks out. To prevent the dissipation of these gradients (which would lead to $V_m = 0$), the cell uses active transport.
- **$Na^+/K^+$-ATPase:** An electrogenic pump that moves $3 Na^+$ out for every $2 K^+$ in.
- **Electrogenic Effect:** Because it moves more positive charge out than in, the pump itself contributes a few millivolts (approx. $-5$ to $-10\text{ mV}$) to the negativity of the resting potential.
    
---

### **V. Experimental Evidence: The Giant Squid Axon**
- **Hodgkin and Huxley (1939):** Used the squid giant axon ($0.5–1\text{ mm}$ diameter) to record the first intracellular action potentials.
- **Verification of GHK:** They demonstrated that changing extracellular $[K^+]$ shifted $V_m$ exactly as predicted by the GHK equation, proving that the resting membrane is primarily a "potassium electrode."

### **Part 1: Learning Objectives**

#### **1. Electrons and ions: how currents flow in solutions**
In metallic conductors, current is carried by the movement of electrons. In biological systems and aqueous solutions, current is carried by **ions** (charged atoms or molecules).
- **Cations** (positive) move toward the cathode (negative pole).
- **Anions** (negative) move toward the anode (positive pole).
    The ease with which these ions move depends on their **mobility ($u$)**, which is influenced by the ion's size, its hydration shell, and the viscosity of the solvent.
    
#### **2. Generation of diffusion and membrane potentials (The Nernst Equation)**
A **diffusion potential** arises when a concentration gradient exists for a salt whose ions have different mobilities (e.g., $Cl^-$ moves faster than $Na^+$). This creates a temporary separation of charge.
A **membrane potential** is a stable version of this, occurring when a membrane is selectively permeable to only certain ions.
- **The Nernst Equation:** Calculates the equilibrium potential ($E_{ion}$) where the chemical diffusion force is exactly balanced by the electrical force.
    - **Formula:** $E_{ion} = \frac{RT}{zF} \ln \frac{[Ion]_{out}}{[Ion]_{in}}$
    - **Significance of terms:** $R$ (Gas constant), $T$ (Absolute temperature), $z$ (Valence/charge of the ion), $F$ (Faraday constant).
        
#### **3. The ionic and molecular basis of the resting membrane potential**
The resting membrane potential ($V_m$) is not at the equilibrium of a single ion but is a **steady-state** determined by:
- **Differential Permeabilities:** At rest, the membrane is much more permeable to $K^+$ than to $Na^+$ or $Cl^-$ (due to leak channels). Therefore, $V_m$ sits closest to $E_K$.
- **The GHK Equation:** $V_m$ is calculated by weighing the concentration gradients of $K^+$, $Na^+$, and $Cl^-$ by their relative permeabilities ($P$).
- **Active Transport:** The $Na^+/K^+$ pump maintains these gradients by using ATP to pump $3 Na^+$ out and $2 K^+$ in.
    
#### **4. How membrane potential is measured**
Membrane potential is measured using **intracellular microelectrodes**. A fine glass pipette filled with a conducting solution (like $KCl$) is inserted into the cell, while a reference electrode is placed in the extracellular fluid. The difference in electrical potential between these two points is recorded by a high-input impedance amplifier.

### **Part 2: Example Questions**

#### **Q1: What is an electrochemical potential?**
The electrochemical potential ($\mu$) is the total free energy available to an ion. It is the sum of:
1. **Chemical Potential:** The energy derived from the concentration gradient ($RT \ln [C]$).
2. **Electrical Potential:** The energy derived from the ion's charge interacting with the membrane voltage ($zFE$).
    Ions always move from a region of high electrochemical potential to a region of low electrochemical potential.
    
#### **Q2: Briefly describe the actions of the $Na^+/K^+$ pump. What determines the $[Na^+]$ gradient it can produce?**
The $Na^+/K^+$-ATPase is an integral membrane protein that:
1. Binds $3 Na^+$ ions from the cytoplasm.
2. Hydrolyzes 1 molecule of ATP to phosphorylate itself, causing a conformational change that releases $Na^+$ outside.
3. Binds $2 K^+$ ions from the outside.
4. Dephosphorylates, returning to the original shape and releasing $K^+$ inside.
    The gradient it can produce is determined by the **energy released from ATP hydrolysis** vs. the **work required** to move the ions against their existing electrochemical gradients.
    
#### **Q3: Describe the properties of a biological membrane that determine whether a molecule can cross it.**
- **Lipid Solubility (Hydrophobicity):** Small, non-polar molecules (like $O_2$ or steroids) dissolve in the lipid and cross via simple diffusion.
- **Charge and Hydration:** Polar or charged molecules (ions) cannot cross the hydrophobic core easily because they are surrounded by water (hydration shells).
- **Presence of Transport Proteins:** For charged molecules, crossing depends on the presence of specific **ion channels** (pore-forming) or **transporters** (binding-based).

#### **Q4: Explain the origin of membrane potential in unexcitable cells.**
In unexcitable cells (like glia or red blood cells), the membrane potential originates primarily from the **high resting permeability to $K^+$**. Because $K^+$ is high inside, it leaks out down its concentration gradient, leaving behind uncompensated negative charges (anions like proteins). This creates an electrical field (negative inside) that eventually pulls $K^+$ back in. When the outward diffusion equals the inward electrical pull, $V_m$ reaches the **Nernst equilibrium for $K^+$**.
