
### **I. Fundamentals of Active Transport**

**1. Definition**
Active transport is the movement of substances across biological membranes **against (up) their electrochemical gradient**. This process is endergonic and requires an external power source.

**2. Power Sources**
- **Primary Active Transport:** Directly utilizes energy from **ATP hydrolysis** (e.g., $Na^+/K^+$ pump, $Ca^{2+}$-ATPase).
- **Secondary Active Transport:** Utilizes the potential energy stored in **ion gradients** (usually $Na^+$) established by primary transporters (e.g., $Na^+/Ca^{2+}$ exchanger, $Na^+/H^+$ exchanger).

**3. Functions**
- Establishing transmembrane ion gradients
- pH regulation
- Solute accumulation
- Termination of synaptic transmission by removing NT
- Second mesenger regulation

---

### **II. Major Transport Systems and Their Mechanics**

#### **1. The $Na^+/K^+$-ATPase (The "Sodium Pump")**
- **Stoichiometry:** Pumps **$3 Na^+$ out** and **$2 K^+$ in** per ATP hydrolyzed.
- **Electrogenicity:** It moves a net of one positive charge out of the cell, making it **electrogenic**. This contributes directly to the negative resting membrane potential.
- **Energy Balance:** ATP hydrolysis provides $\sim 50 \text{ kJ/mole}$. The energy cost to move $3 Na^+$ out and $2 K^+$ in against their respective gradients is $\sim 45 \text{ kJ/mole}$, meaning the pump is highly efficient.
    
#### **2. Calcium Regulation ($Ca^{2+}$)**
Intracellular $[Ca^{2+}]_i$ must be kept extremely low (~100 nM) to allow for its use as a second messenger.
- **$Ca^{2+}$-ATPase (PMCA):** High affinity, low capacity. Directly uses ATP to pump $Ca^{2+}$ out.
- **$Na^+/Ca^{2+}$ Exchanger (NCX):** Low affinity, high capacity. Uses the $Na^+$ gradient to move **$1 Ca^{2+}$ out for $3 Na^+$ in**.
    - **Stoichiometry Note:** Because it moves $3 Na^+$ ($+3$) for $1 Ca^{2+}$ ($+2$), it is electrogenic and its activity is sensitive to $V_m$.
    
#### **3. pH Regulation ($H^+$)**
Metabolism produces acid, which must be expelled to maintain $pH_i \approx 7.0$ (despite a passive equilibrium predicting $pH_i \approx 6.4$ at $V_m = -60 \text{ mV}$).
- **$Na^+/H^+$ Exchanger:** Uses the $Na^+$ gradient to expel $H^+$. It is electroneutral (1 $Na^+$ for 1 $H^+$).
- **$Cl^-/HCO_3^-$ Exchanger:** Regulates bicarbonate levels to buffer intracellular pH.

### **III. Functional Applications in Neurotransmission**

**1. Termination of Synaptic Transmission**
Neurotransmitters must be cleared from the synaptic cleft to prevent excitotoxicity and allow for high-frequency signaling.
- **Mechanism:** Secondary active transport using $Na^+$ and $K^+$ gradients.
- **Example (GABA/Glutamate):** Transporters often couple the entry of $2 Na^+$ and $1 Cl^-$ with the entry of the neurotransmitter.
- **Energy Calculation:** This coupling provides enough energy ($~23.5 \text{ kJ/mole}$) to maintain a **$10^4$-fold gradient** (e.g., $10 \text{ mM}$ inside vs. $1 \mu\text{M}$ outside).
    
**2. Solute Accumulation**
Transporters use the $Na^+$ gradient to concentrate essential nutrients like glucose and lactate inside the cell against their concentration gradients.

### **V. Clinical/Pathological Relevance**

- **Ischemia/Stroke:** Loss of ATP leads to $Na^+/K^+$ pump failure. The $Na^+$ gradient dissipates, causing the $Na^+/Ca^{2+}$ exchanger to run in **reverse** ($Ca^{2+}$ influx), and neurotransmitter transporters to fail, leading to glutamate-mediated excitotoxicity and cell death.

### **Part 1: Learning Objectives**

**1. Define active transport and distinguish between primary and secondary active transport.**
Active transport is the movement of a substance across a biological membrane against its electrochemical gradient. This process is endergonic and requires an external energy source.
- **Primary Active Transport:** Utilizes energy directly from the hydrolysis of ATP. The transporter itself is an ATPase (e.g., the $Na^+/K^+$ pump).
- **Secondary Active Transport:** Utilizes the potential energy stored in an existing ion gradient (most commonly $Na^+$) established by a primary pump. It couples the "downhill" movement of one ion to the "uphill" movement of another substance (e.g., the $Na^+/Ca^{2+}$ exchanger).
    

**2. Explain the energetic requirements for transporting ions against concentration and electrical gradients.**
To move one mole of a substance up a concentration gradient, the required energy is calculated by:
$$\Delta G_{chem} = RT \cdot \ln(C_2/C_1)$$
At physiological temperatures, this equates to approximately **$5.9 \text{ kJ/mole}$** per 10-fold change in concentration.
If the substance is charged ($z$), one must also account for the electrical work required to move it against the membrane potential ($V_m$):
$$\Delta G_{elect} = z \cdot F \cdot V_m$$
For a typical $V_m$ of $-60 \text{ mV}$, moving a univalent cation into the cell requires an additional **$5.8 \text{ kJ/mole}$** of energy. The total energy required is the sum of these chemical and electrical components.

**3. Describe the stoichiometry and physiological role of the $Na^+/K^+$ pump.**
The $Na^+/K^+$-ATPase is the primary determinant of intracellular ion concentrations.
- **Stoichiometry:** It exports **$3 Na^+$** and imports **$2 K^+$** for every molecule of ATP hydrolyzed.
- **Physiological Roles:**
    1. **Gradient Maintenance:** It establishes the high $[K^+]_i$ and low $[Na^+]_i$ necessary for excitability.
    2. **Volume Regulation:** By controlling the internal osmotic pressure, it prevents cell swelling.
    3. **Electrogenicity:** Because it moves a net of one positive charge out of the cell, it contributes directly to the negative resting potential.
        
**4. Understand the mechanisms of $Ca^{2+}$ and pH regulation in neurons.**
- **$Ca^{2+}$ Regulation:** Intracellular $Ca^{2+}$ is kept at $\sim 100 \text{ nM}$ through the **PMCA** ($Ca^{2+}$-ATPase), which has high affinity for fine-tuning, and the **NCX** ($Na^+/Ca^{2+}$ exchanger), which has high capacity for rapid removal following activity.
- **pH Regulation:** Neurons must expel $H^+$ to maintain a $pH_i$ of $\sim 7.0$ (neutral) against a passive equilibrium that would favor a more acidic $pH_i$ of $6.4$. This is achieved via the **$Na^+/H^+$ exchanger** and **$Na^+$-dependent $HCO_3^-/Cl^-$ exchange**.

### **Part 2: Example Questions**

**Q1: How do cells use ion gradients to terminate synaptic transmission?**
Cells terminate transmission by clearing neurotransmitters (like glutamate or GABA) from the synaptic cleft using secondary active transporters. These proteins couple the entry of the neurotransmitter to the entry of $Na^+$ (and sometimes $Cl^-$) down their respective gradients.
- **Example:** Glutamate transporters (EAATs) couple the entry of $3 Na^+$ and $1 H^+$ with the exit of $1 K^+$. This massive thermodynamic drive allows the cell to maintain a $10^6$-fold gradient, keeping extracellular glutamate at sub-micromolar levels to prevent excitotoxicity.
    
**Q2: Calculate the energy required to move GABA into a glial cell if the gradient is 10,000-fold.**
The energy needed to move GABA up a concentration gradient is:
$$\Delta G = RT \cdot \ln([GABA]_i/[GABA]_o)$$
Given that a 10-fold gradient requires $5.9 \text{ kJ/mole}$, a $10^4$ (10,000-fold) gradient requires:
$$4 \times 5.9 \text{ kJ/mole} = 23.6 \text{ kJ/mole}$$
This energy is typically provided by the co-transport of $2 Na^+$ and $1 Cl^-$, which yields approximately $23.5 \text{ kJ/mole}$ from the $Na^+$ and $Cl^-$ gradients and the membrane potential.

**Q3: Discuss the consequences of $Na^+/K^+$ pump failure during cerebral ischemia.**
During ischemia (stroke), ATP production ceases. The $Na^+/K^+$ pump fails, leading to:
1. **Depolarization:** Loss of the $K^+$ gradient and the pump's electrogenic contribution causes $V_m$ to collapse toward zero.
2. **Reverse Transport:** The $Na^+$ gradient dissipates. This causes the $Na^+/Ca^{2+}$ exchanger to run in **reverse**, pumping $Ca^{2+}$ _into_ the cell.
3. **Excitotoxicity:** Neurotransmitter transporters fail or reverse, dumping glutamate into the extracellular space. The resulting massive $Ca^{2+}$ influx triggers proteases and cell death.
