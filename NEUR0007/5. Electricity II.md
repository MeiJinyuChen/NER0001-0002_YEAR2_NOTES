
**I. The Neuron as an Electrical Equivalent Circuit**

The passive electrical behavior of a neuron is defined by three structural features: ion channels (conductors/resistors), concentration gradients (batteries), and the lipid bilayer (capacitor).

**1. Fundamental Electrical Definitions**
- **Potential Difference ($V$ or $E$):** The work required to move a unit of positive charge (1 Coulomb) between two points. Measured in Volts (V).
- **Current ($I$):** The rate of movement of charge ($dQ/dt$). Measured in Amperes (A). In biological systems, current is the movement of ions.
- **Resistance ($R$):** The opposition to current flow ($V/I$). Measured in Ohms ($\Omega$).
- **Conductance ($G$):** The ease with which current flows ($1/R$). Measured in Siemens (S).
    
**2. Ohm’s Law and the Driving Force**
- **Ohm’s Law:** $V = I \cdot R$ or $I = G \cdot V$.
- **Biological Application:** For a specific ion, the current is proportional to the "driving force," which is the difference between the membrane potential and that ion's equilibrium potential.
- **Formula:** $I_{ion} = g_{ion} \cdot (V_m - E_{ion})$.

**II. Membrane Capacitance and the Time Course of Signaling**

The lipid bilayer acts as a capacitor ($C_m$) because it separates and stores charge.

**1. Capacitance Fundamentals**
- **Capacitance ($C$):** $C = Q/V$. The biological constant is roughly $1\text{ }\mu\text{F/cm}^2$.
- **Capacitive Current ($I_c$):** $I_c = C_m \cdot (dV/dt)$. This current flows only when the voltage is changing.
- Because most membranes have about the same thickness, the main factor that makes the capacitance of one neuron different from that of another is the surface area of the neuron. The larger the area, the larger the capacitance of the membrane, and the larger the number of ions that the membrane will be able to store.
    
**2. Total Membrane Current**
The total current ($I_m$) crossing the membrane is the sum of the ionic (resistive) current and the capacitive current.
- **Formula:** $I_m = I_{ion} + I_c = (V_m / R_m) + C_m \cdot (dV/dt)$.
    
**3. The Membrane Time Constant ($\tau$)**
When a step current is applied, the voltage does not change instantly because the capacitor must charge first.
- **Formula:** $\tau = R \cdot C$.
- **Significance:** $\tau$ is the time taken for the voltage to reach 63% of its final steady-state value ($V_\infty$) or decay to 37% of its initial value. Influences the spatial summation and the propagation of AP.
- **Voltage Rise Formula:** $V(t) = V_\infty \cdot (1 - e^{-t/\tau})$.
- **Voltage Decay Formula:** $V(t) = V_0 \cdot e^{-t/\tau}$.

**III. Cable Theory and the Spatial Spread of Signals**

Passive signals (graded potentials) decrease in amplitude as they travel along a neurite (axon or dendrite) due to internal resistance and membrane leakage.

**1. The Membrane Space Constant ($\lambda$)**
The space constant defines the efficiency of passive electronic spread.
- **Formula:** $\lambda = \sqrt{r_m / r_i}$ (where $r_m$ is membrane resistance and $r_i$ is axial/internal resistance).
- **Relationship to Diameter:** Since $r_m \propto 1/d$ and $r_i \propto 1/d^2$, $\lambda \propto \sqrt{d}$. Larger diameter fibers have longer space constants and faster conduction.
- **Voltage Decay with Distance:** $V(x) = V_0 \cdot e^{-x/\lambda}$.

**2. Factors Affecting $\lambda$**
- **Myelination:** Increases $r_m$, thereby increasing $\lambda$ and allowing signals to spread further without active regeneration.
- **Internal Resistance ($r_i$):** Determined by the resistivity of the cytoplasm ($\rho$) and the cross-sectional area.
---

**IV. Temporal and Spatial Summation**
    
Passive properties determine how a neuron integrates multiple inputs (Synaptic Integration).
- **Temporal Summation:** Longer $\tau$ allows consecutive inputs to "piggyback" on the decaying tail of the previous signal.
- **Spatial Summation:** Longer $\lambda$ allows inputs arriving at different locations on the dendrite to reach the trigger zone (axon hillock) with sufficient amplitude to trigger an action potential.

---

**V. Summary of Passive vs. Active Potentials**
- **Passive (Graded) Potentials:** Non-regenerative, propagate with decrement (exponential decay), and can summate. Their spread is governed entirely by $\tau$ and $\lambda$.
- **Active (Action) Potentials:** Regenerative, propagate without decrement, and follow the all-or-none law (cannot summate).

### **Part 1: Learning Objectives**

**1. The electrical equivalent circuit of the membrane and its utility.**
A neuron's membrane is modeled as an electrical circuit where:
- **Capacitor ($C_m$):** Represents the lipid bilayer, which stores charge.
- **Resistors/Conductors ($R_m$ or $G_m$):** Represent ion channels that allow current to flow.
- **Batteries ($E_{ion}$):** Represent the equilibrium potentials (Nernst potentials) created by concentration gradients.
    **Utility:** These circuits provide a quantitative way to predict how a neuron will respond to electrical inputs and how signals will propagate or decay.
    
**2. Basic ionic mechanisms of receptor and synaptic potentials.**

Receptor and synaptic potentials are "graded potentials" caused by the opening or closing of specific ion channels in response to a stimulus (like light, pressure, or a neurotransmitter).

- **Excitatory (EPSP):** Typically involves increasing conductance to $Na^+$ or non-selective cations, moving $V_m$ toward a more positive threshold.
    
- **Inhibitory (IPSP):** Typically involves increasing conductance to $K^+$ or $Cl^-$, moving $V_m$ away from the threshold.
    
**3. How membrane capacitance ($C_m$) and resistance ($R_m$) affect the time course of signals.**

The product of these two values defines the **Membrane Time Constant ($\tau = R_m \cdot C_m$)**.

- **Time Course:** $\tau$ is the time it takes for a voltage change to reach 63% of its maximum value.
    
- **Integration:** A larger $\tau$ means the membrane charges and discharges slowly, allowing for greater **temporal summation** (consecutive inputs can add together).
    
**4. How membrane and cytoplasmic (axial) resistance affect signal conduction.**

The efficiency of signal spread over distance is defined by the **Space Constant ($\lambda = \sqrt{r_m / r_i}$)**.

- **$r_m$ (Membrane Resistance):** Higher $r_m$ (fewer open channels) prevents current leak, increasing $\lambda$.
    
- **$r_i$ (Internal/Axial Resistance):** Lower $r_i$ (larger diameter) allows current to flow more easily down the axon, increasing $\lambda$.
    
**5. How passive properties and axon diameter affect threshold and conduction velocity.**

- **Threshold:** Neurons with larger $\lambda$ and $\tau$ are easier to bring to threshold because the signal spreads further and lasts longer.
    
- **Velocity:** Conduction velocity is proportional to the space constant. Larger diameter axons have higher $\lambda$, meaning the depolarization spreads further ahead of the action potential, allowing it to "jump" faster. Myelination increases $r_m$ dramatically, further boosting velocity through saltatory conduction.

### **Part 2: Example Questions**

**Q1: Explain the roles played by membrane capacitance and resistance in determining the time course and spatial spread of electrical signals.**

- **Time Course:** Resistance ($R_m$) and capacitance ($C_m$) determine the time constant ($\tau = RC$). This dictates the rate of voltage rise and decay. Without capacitance, the voltage would change instantaneously with current; because of it, signals are smoothed out over time.
    
- **Spatial Spread:** The ratio of membrane resistance ($r_m$) to axial resistance ($r_i$) determines the space constant ($\lambda$). This dictates how far a signal travels before it decays to 37% of its original value. Higher $r_m$ and lower $r_i$ optimize spread.
    
**Q2: Comparing Synapses A and B on dendrites of different diameters.**

- **Scenario:** Dendrite 1 has a greater diameter than Dendrite 2.
    
- **Answer:** The input from **Dendrite 1** will be larger at the trigger zone.
    
- **Reasoning:** A larger diameter results in a lower internal resistance ($r_i$). Since $\lambda = \sqrt{r_m / r_i}$, a lower $r_i$ increases the space constant ($\lambda$). A larger $\lambda$ means the signal decays less as it travels the fixed distance to the trigger zone.
    
**Q3: How will an increase in membrane capacitance affect length constant, conduction velocity, and synaptic potential duration?**

- **i) Length Constant ($\lambda$):** No effect. $\lambda$ is determined by $r_m$ and $r_i$, not capacitance.
    
- **ii) Conduction Velocity:** Decreases. A higher capacitance means it takes more time and charge to change the voltage of the next segment of membrane to threshold.
    
- **iii) Duration of Synaptic Potentials:** Increases. Since $\tau = R_m \cdot C_m$, increasing $C$ increases the time constant, meaning the synaptic potential will take longer to decay back to baseline.

### **I. The Cable Model (Continuous Core Conductor Theory)**

The **Cable Model** (pioneered by Wilfrid Rall using mathematical concepts originally developed by Lord Kelvin for transatlantic telegraph cables) treats neuronal processes—such as dendrites and unmyelinated axons—as infinitely long, uniform, continuous cylindrical cables.

#### **1. Structural Components and Electrical Correspondences**

Instead of assuming that voltage is uniform throughout the entire cell (as in a single-point spherical model), the cable model introduces lateral physical dimensions, accounting for internal resistance along the length of the process.

- **Extracellular Fluid $\rightarrow$ Outside Conductor ($r_o$):** The fluid outside the cell is highly conductive and typically assumed to be so vast that its longitudinal resistance ($r_o$) is negligible or zero.
    
- **Intracellular Cytoplasm $\rightarrow$ Axial / Longitudinal Resistance ($r_a$ or $r_i$):** The narrow internal core of the axon or dendrite contains ions but presents a physical barrier to longitudinal current flow due to its small cross-sectional area. This is modeled as a <span style="background:#fff88f">continuous series of internal resistors</span> ($r_a$, measured in $\Omega/\text{cm}$).
    
- **Insulating Lipid Bilayer $\rightarrow$ Membrane Capacitance ($c_m$):** The continuous surface area of the cylindrical plasma membrane acts as a capacitor ($c_m$, measured in $\mu\text{F/cm}$), storing electrical charge along the length of the cable.
    
- **Open Ion Channels $\rightarrow$ Membrane Resistance / Conductance ($r_m$ or $g_m$):** The resting leak channels distributed continuously along the cylinder wall represent the membrane resistance ($r_m$, measured in $\Omega\cdot\text{cm}$), regulating how much current leaks out into the extracellular space.
    
#### **2. Core Biophysical Parameters**

The continuous cable model defines how an electrical signal (like a post-synaptic potential) decays over space and time via two critical constants:

- **The Membrane Time Constant ($\tau_m = r_m \cdot c_m$):** Dictates how rapidly the membrane capacitor charges or discharges over time at any single point along the cable.
    
- **The Length Constant ($\lambda$):** Defines the distance over which a steady-state voltage injection decays to $37\%$ ($1/e$) of its original maximum amplitude ($V_0$). It is mathematically defined as:
    
    $$\lambda = \sqrt{\frac{r_m}{r_a}}$$
    
    - **Anatomical Significance:** Because $r_m \propto 1/\text{diameter}$ and $r_a \propto 1/\text{diameter}^2$, the formula simplifies to show that **$\lambda \propto \sqrt{\text{diameter}}$**. Therefore, thicker dendrites or axons have a larger length constant, allowing electrical currents to propagate much further with less spatial attenuation.

### **II. The Compartmental Model (Discrete Discretized Architecture)**

While the continuous cable model works perfectly for ideal, unbranching cylinders, real central neurons possess highly complex, asymmetrical geometries with tapering diameters, extensive dendritic branching, and thousands of localized dendritic spines. To solve this, neurophysiologists use the **Compartmental Model**.

#### **1. The Principle of Discretization**

The compartmental model mathematically slices a complex neuron into <span style="background:#fff88f">multiple small, discrete, interconnected structural functional pieces called compartments.</span>

- Within any single compartment, the physical dimensions (diameter, length) are chosen to be small enough that **electrical voltage ($V$) is assumed to be spatially uniform** throughout that segment.
    
- Spatial variation across the neuron only occurs _between_ different compartments.
    
#### **2. Structural Components and Electrical Correspondences**

<span style="background:#fff88f">Each single compartment is modeled as its own localized parallel RC circuit, which is then linked to neighboring compartments via an axial resistor:</span>

- **Local Membrane Surface Area $\rightarrow$ Compartmental Capacitor ($C_m$):** Represents the localized ability of that specific patch of lipid bilayer to store charge.
    
- **Local Ion Channels $\rightarrow$ Compartmental Resistors / Conductors ($R_m$ or $g_m$):** Represents the specific density of leak, voltage-gated, or ligand-gated channels in that exact patch of membrane.
    
- **Local Reversal Potentials $\rightarrow$ Batteries ($E_{\text{ion}}$):** Represents the thermodynamic driving forces for specific ion species within that distinct compartment.
    
- **The Cytoplasmic Link between Compartments $\rightarrow$ Axial Coupling Resistors ($R_A$):** Represents the internal physical resistance encountered by current as it flows from the center of one discrete compartment (e.g., a dendritic spine head) into the center of an adjacent compartment (e.g., the dendritic shaft).

### **III. Summary Comparison for Exam Synthesis**

|**Biophysical Property**|**Cable Model**|**Compartmental Model**|
|---|---|---|
|**Mathematical Nature**|**Continuous** (Uses partial differential equations to track calculus changes over space $x$ and time $t$).|**Discrete** (Uses matrices of ordinary differential equations to track distinct nodes).|
|**Morphological Idealization**|Restricted to uniform, straight, infinitely long cylinders.|Can replicate highly complex, asymmetric geometries, branches, and spines.|
|**Membrane Properties**|Assumed to be strictly **passive** (linear leak conductances only).|Can be **active** (investigators can insert non-linear, voltage-gated Hodgkin-Huxley currents into specific compartments like the axon initial segment).|
|**Longitudinal Component**|Represented as an infinitely distributed, infinitesimal series of internal resistors ($r_a$).|Represented as a finite, countable set of discrete coupling resistors ($R_A$) linking specific nodal points.|
