
### **I. The CNS Micro-environment: The Role of the Extracellular Space (ECS)**
- **Physical Constraint:** The ECS is remarkably small (occupying only ~20% of brain volume).
- **Physiological Consequence:** Because the volume is restricted, even minor ionic fluxes (e.g., $K^+$ efflux during an action potential) lead to massive percentage changes in extracellular concentrations.
- **The Glial Solution:** Astrocytes (>50% of brain cells) act as the primary homeostatic buffers to prevent "information mixing" or epileptic discharges caused by these shifts.
    

---

### **II. Extracellular Potassium ($[K^+]_o$) Homeostasis**
Neuronal activity raises $[K^+]_o$, which would passively depolarize neighboring neurons. 
#### Result
- Extracellular <font color="#000000">[K⁺] </font>rises
- Nearby neurons depolarise
- Mixing of the information coded by the two neurons
- Severe rise → epileptic discharge

Astrocytes counteract this via two mechanisms:
 **1. Passive Spatial Buffering (through Ion Channels)**
 **2. Active Removal (through $Na^+/K^+$ Pump)**

#### Control of Uniform <font color="#000000">[K<sup>+</sup>]</font>o Rise
##### Process
- Astrocyte membrane is K<sup>+</sup> specific → Depolarises toward new Eₖ
- (Almost) no K⁺ flow through ion channels
- K<sup>+</sup> uptake occur only via the Na⁺/K⁺ pump
##### Key limitation:
- Pump effectiveness depends on saturation state
#### Control of Localised <font color="#000000">[K<sup>+</sup>]</font>o Rise = Spatial Buffering
##### Process
- Astrocyte depolarisation to a new resting potential that is an average of the value of E<sub>K</sub> all over the cell
- Local high-K⁺ regions:
    - K⁺ influx
- Distal low-K⁺ regions:
    - K⁺ efflux
##### Result
Spatial buffering
- Redistributes K⁺ away from active regions
- Prevents local hyperexcitability
- Maintains extracellular ionic stability

#### Mechanisms Facilitating Spatial Buffering
##### 1. Gap Junction Coupling
- Astrocytes interconnected in syncytium
- K⁺ disperses across astrocyte network
##### 2. High K⁺ Conductance in Endfeet
- Astrocytic endfeet contact blood vessels
- > 90% of K⁺ conductance concentrated here
- Directs K⁺ disposal into blood or vitreous humour

Unlike acetylcholine:
- No extracellular enzyme degrades glutamate
#### Process
1. Synaptically released glutamate taken into astrocytes by EAAT
2. Converted to glutamine by glutamine synthetase
3. Glutamine returned to neurons
4. Reconverted to glutamate

### **IV. Regulation of Brain Blood Flow (Neurovascular Coupling)**

Astrocytes act as a bridge between active synapses and blood vessels (capillaries).

- **Sensing Activity:** Neuronal release of Glutamate activates metabotropic glutamate receptors (**mGluRs**) on astrocytes, causing a rise in intracellular calcium ($[Ca^{2+}]_i$).
    
- **The Signaling Pathway:** 1. Rise in $[Ca^{2+}]_i$ activates **Phospholipase A2 (PLA2)**.
    
    2. PLA2 produces **Arachidonic Acid (AA)**.
    
    3. AA is converted by **COX-1** into **Prostaglandins (PG)**.
    
- **The Effector:** Prostaglandins diffuse to smooth muscle cells or **pericytes** on capillaries, causing them to relax and the vessels to **dilate**. This increases local blood flow to meet the metabolic demand of active neurons.

### **V. Linkage to Experimental Methods (Exam Focus)**

To provide high-level evidence in your exam, reference the following techniques used to study these glial processes:

**1. Ratiometric Calcium Imaging (Fura-2 / Fluo-4)**

- **Application:** Used to demonstrate that stimulating a neuron (releasing glutamate) triggers a $Ca^{2+}$ rise in the astrocyte endfoot before the adjacent capillary dilates.
    
- **Evidence:** This proves that the astrocyte is an active signaling intermediary rather than just a passive support cell.
    

**2. Pharmacological Blockade of Dilations**

- **Method:** Use of **COX-1 inhibitors** (e.g., indomethacin) or **mGluR antagonists**.
    
- **Logic:** If vessel dilation in response to neuronal activity is abolished by these drugs, it confirms the Prostaglandin-mediated pathway identified in the slides.
    

**3. BAPTA vs. EGTA Dialysis**

- **Method:** Infusing astrocytes with **BAPTA** (a fast $Ca^{2+}$ chelator) via a patch pipette.
    
- **Result:** BAPTA prevents the rise in astrocyte $[Ca^{2+}]_i$ and subsequently blocks neurovascular coupling. This provides causal evidence that the calcium rise is necessary for blood flow regulation.
    

**4. Ion-Selective Microelectrodes (ISMs)**

- **Method:** Measuring $[K^+]_o$ directly in the ECS during stimulation.
    
- **Result:** If $[K^+]_o$ rises significantly higher in the presence of **Barium ($Ba^{2+}$)** (which blocks Kir channels) or gap junction blockers, it provides quantitative proof of the **Spatial Buffer Mechanism**.
    

**5. Stroke/Ischemia Simulation**

- **Link to Paper:** In the "Zinc" or "YM023" questions, remember that **ATP depletion** halts the $Na^+/K^+$ pump and Glutamine Synthetase.
    
- **Prediction:** The resulting failure of glutamate uptake and $K^+$ buffering leads to a positive feedback loop: High $[K^+]_o$ and Glutamate $\rightarrow$ Neuronal Depolarization $\rightarrow$ More Release $\rightarrow$ **Spreading Depression** and widespread cell death.

### **Part 1: Learning Objectives**

#### **1. Understand mechanisms by which the composition of the micro-environment in the CNS is altered by neural activity in both normal and pathological situations.**
- **Normal Physiology:** During action potential propagation and synaptic transmission, neurons release potassium ($K^+$) into the extracellular space (ECS) during repolarization, and accumulate sodium ($Na^+$). Concurrently, excitatory synapses release neurotransmitters like glutamate into the cleft.
- **Pathological Situations:** Under high-frequency firing (e.g., epilepsy) or energy failure (e.g., ischemia/stroke), the local clearance systems are overwhelmed. Intracellular ATP depletion halts primary active transport, causing a massive efflux of $K^+$ and an accumulation of extracellular glutamate. This triggers sustained neuronal depolarization, opening voltage-gated $Ca^{2+}$ channels and NMDA receptors, culminating in calcium-mediated excitotoxicity and cell death.
    
#### **2. Understand that the extracellular space is small, and the implications of this.**
- **The Constraint:** The ECS accounts for only approximately **20% of the total brain volume**.
- **The Implications:** Because this volume fraction is extremely restricted, even small absolute molar fluxes of ions across neuronal membranes translate into large percentage changes in extracellular concentrations. For instance, unbuffered $K^+$ accumulation would quickly shift the Nernst equilibrium potential ($E_K$) in a positive direction, depolarizing neighboring unexcited neurons. This leads to cross-talk ("information mixing") between independent neural pathways or pathologically synchronized firing (epileptic discharges).
    
#### **3. Understand how the ionic composition of the micro-environment can be measured experimentally with ion-selective microelectrodes and how both these measurements and glial membrane potentials change during disturbances.**
- **Ion-Selective Microelectrodes (ISMs):** ISMs consist of a double-barreled glass pipette. One barrel acts as a conventional microelectrode to measure the local extracellular field potential ($V_e$). The tip of the second barrel contains a liquid membrane ionophore selective for a specific ion (e.g., $K^+$). The potential difference between the two barrels isolates the concentration-dependent electrochemical voltage, allowing real-time quantification of extracellular ion levels.
- **Glial Membrane Potential Changes:** Astrocytes possess an exceptionally high resting permeability to $K^+$ governed by inwardly rectifying $K^+$ channels (Kir). Consequently, the glial membrane potential ($V_g$) acts as a natural "potassium electrode" and accurately tracks the local Nernst potential for $K^+$:
    $$\Delta V_{g} \approx 58 \cdot \log\left(\frac{[K^+]_{o\text{, new}}}{[K^+]_{o\text{, rest}}}\right)$$
    During pathological disturbances like epilepsy or spreading depression, ISMs record a sharp rise in $[K^+]_o$ (up to 12–40 mM), which is mirrored by a significant, prolonged depolarization of the recorded glial cells.
    

#### **4. Understand how glial cells remove neurotransmitter from the extracellular space.**
- **Glutamate Uptake via EAATs:** Astrocytes utilize high-affinity Excitatory Amino Acid Transporters (EAAT1/2) to clear synaptic glutamate.
- **Stoichiometric Energetics:** This secondary active transport process couples the influx of **1 glutamate molecule**, **3 $Na^+$ ions**, and **1 $H^+$ ion** to the efflux of **1 $K^+$ ion**.
    
#### **5. Understand how positive feedback processes can lead to severe dysfunction of neural tissue (e.g., Spreading Depression, Stroke) and how these are countered by glial homeostatic mechanisms.**
- **Pathological Positive Feedback:** If $[K^+]_o$ rises above a critical threshold (~12 mM), it depolarizes adjacent neurons, inducing further voltage-gated $K^+$ efflux and glutamate release. This creates a self-propagating wave of cellular depolarization and metabolic depression known as **Spreading Depression**. In a stroke, the absolute lack of ATP completely stops clearance, locked in a terminal positive feedback loop.
- **Glial Counter-Mechanisms:** Glia break this cycle through three main pathways:
    1. **$K^+$ Spatial Buffer Mechanism:** A passive pathway where local $K^+$ entry into highly permeable astrocytes creates an intracellular current loop through the gap-junction-coupled glial syncytium, safely dumping $K^+$ at distant sites with lower concentrations.
    2. **Active $K^+$ Sequestration:** Up-regulation of glial $Na^+/K^+$-ATPases to actively clear $K^+$ when passive mechanisms are saturated.
    3. **High-Drive Transmitter Uptake:** EAAT activity clears the excitotoxic glutamate driving the neuronal over-excitation.

### **Part 2: Exam-Type Application Questions**

#### **Q1: Explain why dendrite 1 having a larger diameter than dendrite 2 results in a larger synaptic input when recorded at the axon hillock/trigger zone, given they are equidistant and receive identical initial postsynaptic depolarizations.**
**Answer:** The attenuation of a passively propagating electrical signal along a dendrite is governed by its length constant ($\lambda$), which is defined mathematically as:
$$\lambda = \sqrt{\frac{r_m}{r_i}}$$
Where $r_m$ is the membrane resistance per unit length, and $r_i$ is the internal axial resistance per unit length.
When structural dimensions vary, axial resistance is inversely proportional to the cross-sectional area ($r_i \propto 1/d^2$), whereas membrane resistance is inversely proportional to the circumference ($r_m \propto 1/d$). Substituting these proportionalities into the space constant equation demonstrates that $\lambda$ scales proportionally with the square root of the fiber diameter:
$$\lambda \propto \sqrt{d}$$
Because dendrite 1 possesses a larger diameter than dendrite 2, it has a significantly lower axial resistance ($r_i$), yielding a larger length constant ($\lambda_1 > \lambda_2$). According to the steady-state electronic decay equation:
$$V(x) = V_0 \cdot e^{-x/\lambda}$$
the voltage attenuation over the distance $x$ to the trigger zone will be substantially less in dendrite 1. Consequently, the synaptic potential traveling down the wider dendrite experiences less decay and arrives at the trigger zone with a higher amplitude, contributing more effectively to spatial summation.

#### **Q2: Analyze how an increase in the membrane capacitance ($C_m$, capacitance per unit area) of a neuron affects: i) its length constant; ii) its conduction velocity; and iii) the duration of the synaptic potentials generated.**
**Answer:**
- **i) Effect on Length Constant ($\lambda$):** An increase in membrane capacitance has **no effect** on the steady-state length constant. The formula for $\lambda = \sqrt{r_m / r_i}$ is purely a function of steady-state structural resistances ($r_m$ and $r_i$) and is completely independent of capacitive (time-varying) parameters.
- **ii) Effect on Conduction Velocity:** The conduction velocity will **decrease**. Propagation of an action potential or passive electronic spread requires local current circuits to charge up the adjacent, unexcited membrane capacitance to its threshold potential. The time required to alter the membrane voltage is directly restricted by the membrane time constant ($\tau_m = r_m \cdot c_m$). Increasing the capacitance increases $\tau_m$, meaning it takes longer for local circuits to depolarize adjacent patches of membrane, thus slowing down the propagation speed along the process.
- **iii) Effect on Synaptic Potential Duration:** The duration of the synaptic potentials will **increase**. Following the termination of active synaptic current injection, the decay phase of a postsynaptic potential is purely passive and determined by the rate at which the stored capacitive charge discharges back through the resting membrane resistance. This decay is dictated by the time constant $\tau_m$. Because an increase in $C_m$ directly prolongs $\tau_m$, the passive decay phase is extended, creating longer-lasting synaptic potentials that increase the window for temporal summation.
