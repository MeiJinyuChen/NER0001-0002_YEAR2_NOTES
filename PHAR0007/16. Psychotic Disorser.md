
# 1. Schizophrenia 

## 1.1 Symptoms

### Positive symptoms

1. Delusions
2. Hallucinaions
3. Disorganised thinking/speech/behaviour
4. Lack of insight

### Negative symptoms

1. Avolition (lacking motivation)
2. Alogia (reduced speech)
3. Anhedonia (lacking pleasure or interest)
4. Blunted affects (reduced emotional expressiveness)

### Cognitive symptoms

Impairments in:
1. Attention
2. Working memory
3. Learning
4. Motor speed
5. Executive functions
6. ...

## 1.2 Pathophysiology

### General

#### A. Dopamine Hypothesis 

- **↑ DA, especially D₂ pathway → positive symptoms**
- Evidence:
    - Amphetamine ↑ DA → induces psychosis
    - L-DOPA worsens symptoms
    - Antipsychotics (D₂ antagonists) reduce symptoms
	- ↑ DA synthesis in dorsal striatum during psychosis

#### B. Glutamate Hypothesis

- **↓ NMDA function contributes to SCZ**
- Evidence:
	- NMDA antagonists (PCP, ketamine) → psychosis-like symptoms  

**<font color="#00b050">Dopamine Pathways & Clinical Relevance</font>**

|Pathway|Function|Drug Effect|
|---|---|---|
|Mesolimbic|Reward|↑ DA → **positive symptoms**|
|Mesocortical|Cognition|↓ DA → **negative symptoms**|
|Nigrostriatal|Movement|D₂ block → **EPS**|
|Tuberoinfundibular|Prolactin|D₂ block → **↑ prolactin**|

### Positive syptoms

#### Aberrant salience hypothesis

- DA release in dorsal straitum signals salient events in environment
- DA dysregulation in dorsal striatum causes irrelevant environmental stimuli to become stimuli → Delutions

# 2. Treatment

- **Atypicals = first-line**
- Choice depends on:
    - Side effect
    - Cost

## 2.1 First-Generation (Typical) Antipsyhotics

### Drugs

- **Chlorpromazine**, Haloperidol, Perphenazine

### Mechanism

- **Strong D₂ receptor antagonists**  
    → Reduce mesolimbic DA activity
	→ ↓ Positive symptoms 

### Therapeutic Effects

- Improve positive symptoms strongly
- Little effect on negative/cognitive symptoms
- Strong side effects (EPS)

### Major Side Effects

#### 1. Extrapyramidal Symptoms (EPS)

(from nigrostriatal D₂ blockade)

| Type               | Features                                                                                                                                                                  |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Parkinsonism       | Tremor, rigidity                                                                                                                                                          |
| Acute dystonia     | Muscle spasms of tongue, face, neck and back                                                                                                                              |
| Akathisia          | Restless movements                                                                                                                                                        |
| Tardive dyskinesia | Involuntary movements<br><font color="#245bdb">Caused by long-term DA blockade in straitum</font><br><font color="#245bdb">Cannot be treated with anticholinergics</font> |

##### Mechanism:

- Caused by DA-ACh imbalance in basal ganglia
	- ↓ DA → relative ↑ ACh→ motor dysfunction
- Treated with **anticholinergics (e.g., benztropine, diphenhydramine)** 

#### 2. Endocrine

- ↑ Prolactin → galactorrhoea, gynaecomastia

#### 3. Autonomic & Others

- Sedation (H₁ block)
- Orthostatic hypotension (ARα₁ block)
- Anticholinergic effects

## 2.2 Second-Generation (Atypical) Antipsychotics

### Drugs

- **Dibenzapine derivatives: 
  Clozapine, Olanzapine, Quetiapine
- **Benzisoxazole derivatives:**
  **Risperidone**

### Pros and Cons

| Pros                                      | Cons                   |
| ----------------------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Treat both positive and negative symptoms | Also side effects <br> |
| Better treating cognitive symptoms        | Still EPS but fewer    |
| Good at treating Drug-resistant psychosis |                        |
| Fewer EPS / tardive dyskinesia            |                        |

### Mechanism

- **Weak D₂ antagonism + Strong 5-HT<sub>₂A</sub> antagonism**
- Block 5-HT signalling (autoceptors) 
  → ↑ DA transmission
  → (along with weak D2 antagonism) 
  → Balance between DA and 5-HT
  → Less EPS

### Side Effects

| Type      | Details                                                                |
| --------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Metabolic | Weight gain, hypercholesterolaemia, type II diabetes (esp. olanzapine) |
| Autonomic | Postural hypotension                                                   |
| Endocrine | Hyperprolactinaemia                                                    |
| CV        | QT prolongation                                                        |
| Serious   | **Agranulocytosis (clozapine)**                                        |

## 2.3 Third-Generation Antipsychotics

### Drugs

- **Aripiprazole**

### Mechanism 

- **D₂ partial agonist** (high affinity, low intrinsic activity)
- 5-HT<sub>1A</sub> partial agonist
- 5-HT<sub>2A</sub> antagonist
→ “Stabilises” DA (↓ when high, ↑ when low)

### Pros and Cons

| Pros                                   | Cons                        |
| -------------------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Fewer EPS (no difference from placebo) | Still hyperprolactinaemia   |
| Little weight gain & QT elongation     | Still hypercholesterolaemia |
| Safe in overdose                       | Akathisia                   |

# Example Question

## 1. “Describe the dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia and explain how this informs antipsychotic drug therapy.”

The dopamine hypothesis proposes that schizophrenia results primarily from dysregulated dopamine signalling within specific CNS pathways. Hyperactivity of mesolimbic dopamine transmission, particularly excessive D₂ receptor stimulation, is associated with positive symptoms such as hallucinations, delusions, and disorganised thought. Conversely, reduced mesocortical dopamine activity may contribute to negative and cognitive symptoms.

Strong evidence supports this model. Amphetamine increases dopamine release and can induce psychosis, while L-DOPA may worsen psychotic symptoms. Most effective antipsychotic drugs reduce symptoms through D₂ receptor antagonism.

Typical antipsychotics such as haloperidol and chlorpromazine strongly block D₂ receptors, particularly reducing mesolimbic dopamine signalling and effectively improving positive symptoms. However, because D₂ blockade also affects nigrostriatal and tuberoinfundibular pathways, major side effects include extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS), tardive dyskinesia, and hyperprolactinaemia.

Atypical antipsychotics combine weaker D₂ antagonism with strong 5-HT₂A blockade, partially restoring cortical dopamine release while reducing EPS risk. Drugs such as <font color="#c00000">clozapine </font>and<font color="#c00000"> olanzapine </font>therefore improve both positive and some negative symptoms, though metabolic side effects are significant.

Third-generation drugs such as <font color="#c00000">aripiprazole</font> act as D₂ partial agonists, stabilising dopamine transmission and reducing both excessive and deficient signalling.

Thus, dopamine pathway specificity explains both therapeutic effects and side effect profiles of antipsychotics. While dopamine blockade remains central, newer therapies attempt to preserve efficacy while minimising broad dopaminergic suppression.

## 2. “Compare first-, second-, and third-generation antipsychotic drugs.”

Antipsychotic drugs are broadly divided into first-generation (typical), second-generation (atypical), and third-generation agents based on receptor pharmacology and side effect profiles.

First-generation antipsychotics, such as haloperidol and chlorpromazine, primarily act through strong D₂ receptor antagonism. They are highly effective for positive symptoms but have limited efficacy for negative or cognitive symptoms. Major limitations include extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS), tardive dyskinesia, and hyperprolactinaemia due to widespread dopamine blockade.

Second-generation antipsychotics, including clozapine, olanzapine, quetiapine, and risperidone, combine weaker D₂ antagonism with strong 5-HT₂A antagonism. This increases cortical dopamine release while preserving mesolimbic suppression, reducing EPS and improving broader symptom domains. Clozapine is particularly effective in treatment-resistant schizophrenia. However, metabolic syndrome, weight gain, diabetes, QT prolongation, and agranulocytosis remain major concerns.

Third-generation antipsychotics such as aripiprazole function primarily as D₂ partial agonists. Rather than simply blocking dopamine, they stabilise dopamine transmission by reducing excessive signalling while partially preserving deficient pathways. This produces fewer EPS, reduced metabolic burden, and improved overdose safety. However, akathisia and some endocrine effects may still occur.

Overall, drug development has progressively improved tolerability while maintaining antipsychotic efficacy. Choice depends on balancing symptom control, side effect burden, and patient-specific risk factors.
