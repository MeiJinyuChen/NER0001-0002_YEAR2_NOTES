# 2. **Mechanisms**

- Examples of factors causing dysrhythmias

## **2.1 Abnormal impulse formation**
- **Enhanced normal automaticity**
	- ↑ rate of normal pacemaker cells
	- β-AR stimulation
	- Hypokalaemia
	- Acidosis
	- Fibre stretch
- **Abnormal automaticity**
	- Spontaneous firing from cells that should not be pacemakers
- **"Triggered" automaticity**
	- Afterdepolarisation (early/delayed) triggered by preceding activity
	1. EAD - delayed repolarisation
		- long QT syndrome - long duration of ventricular depolarisation
			- Symptoms: syncope/seizures, sudden death caused by specific ventricular dysrhythmia
			- Caused by:
			1. Inherited: loss-of-function mutations in K<sub>DR</sub>
			2. Acquired: exposure to certain drugs, hypokalemia
	2. DAD - high <font color="#000000">[Ca<sup>2+</sup>]</font><sub>i</sub> → high NCX activity → transient inward current → depolarisation
	![[Pasted image 20260422161125.png|250]]
## **2.2 Abnormal impulse conduction**
- **Re-entrant dysrhythmia**
	- normal atrial AP: dies out as impulses converge on AV node
	- damaged atrial muscle → repolarisation phase of a preceding impulse interacts with anterograde impulse → unidirectional block → re-excite tissue
	![[Pasted image 20260422161702.png|250]]
- **Heart block**
	- Failue of impulse generation in SA node / Failure of propagation through AV node
	- Ventricular beating maintained by abnormal pacemaker (slow and unreliable)

# Example Question


## 1. “Describe the major mechanisms responsible for cardiac dysrhythmias and explain how these mechanisms inform therapeutic strategies.”

Cardiac dysrhythmias arise from abnormalities in impulse generation or conduction. Major mechanisms include enhanced automaticity, abnormal automaticity, triggered activity, and re-entry circuits.

Enhanced automaticity occurs when normal pacemaker cells, particularly the sinoatrial node, fire excessively due to β-adrenergic stimulation, hypokalaemia, acidosis, or fibre stretch. Abnormal automaticity involves spontaneous depolarisation in non-pacemaker cells, often in damaged myocardium.

Triggered activity is caused by afterdepolarisations. Early afterdepolarisations (EADs) occur during delayed repolarisation, often in long QT syndrome, and can lead to torsades de pointes. Delayed afterdepolarisations (DADs) result from intracellular Ca²⁺ overload, increasing Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger activity and provoking depolarisation.

Re-entry is a particularly important mechanism in clinical tachyarrhythmias. It occurs when a unidirectional conduction block allows re-excitation of tissue, creating a self-sustaining loop. Re-entry underlies many forms of atrial fibrillation, supraventricular tachycardia, and ventricular tachycardia.

Therapeutic strategies target these mechanisms directly. Na⁺ channel blockers reduce conduction velocity, interrupting re-entry circuits. β-blockers reduce sympathetic stimulation and automaticity. K⁺ channel blockers prolong refractory periods to prevent re-excitation, while Ca²⁺ channel blockers reduce AV nodal conduction.

Drug choice depends on dysrhythmia type, tissue involved, and patient-specific risk factors. However, many antiarrhythmics carry pro-arrhythmic risks, particularly Class I and III drugs.

Overall, understanding underlying electrophysiological disturbances is essential for rational anti-dysrhythmic therapy and balancing therapeutic benefit against significant toxicity.


## 2. “Compare the Vaughan-Williams classes of anti-dysrhythmic drugs, including mechanisms, uses, strengths, and limitations.”

The Vaughan-Williams classification divides anti-dysrhythmic drugs into four major classes based on primary electrophysiological action.

Class I drugs are Na⁺ channel blockers that reduce phase 0 depolarisation and slow conduction. Subclass Ia agents (<font color="#c00000">quinidine</font>, disopyramide) also prolong repolarisation through K⁺ blockade but risk QT prolongation. Class Ib agents (<font color="#c00000">lidocaine</font>) preferentially target <font color="#c00000">depolarised ventricular tissue</font> and are useful in <font color="#c00000">ventricular arrhythmias</font>. Class Ic agents (flecainide) strongly suppress conduction but carry high pro-arrhythmic risk.

Class II drugs are β-blockers such as <font color="#c00000">propranolol and metoprolol</font>. They reduce <font color="#c00000">sympathetic stimulation, suppress SA node firing, and slow AV conduction</font>. These are especially valuable in <font color="#c00000">tachyarrhythmias</font> and post-myocardial infarction due to mortality benefits.

Class III drugs such as amiodarone and <font color="#c00000">sotalol</font> block K⁺ channels,<font color="#c00000"> prolonging repolarisation and refractory period</font>. They are highly effective for both <font color="#c00000">atrial and ventricular dysrhythmias</font> but can cause <font color="#c00000">QT prolongation</font> and serious systemic toxicity.

Class IV drugs (<font color="#c00000">verapamil</font>, diltiazem) block L-type Ca²⁺ channels, reducing SA and AV nodal conduction. They are mainly useful in supraventricular arrhythmias.

Although mechanistically useful, this classification is imperfect because many drugs have mixed actions. For example, amiodarone also blocks Na⁺ and Ca²⁺ channels.

Overall, each class targets distinct electrophysiological abnormalities, but anti-dysrhythmic therapy is limited by toxicity, incomplete selectivity, and pro-arrhythmic potential.


## 3. “Discuss the pharmacological management of atrial fibrillation and ventricular dysrhythmias.”

Atrial fibrillation (AF) and ventricular dysrhythmias differ significantly in pathophysiology and therapeutic urgency.

AF involves rapid, disorganised atrial activity, often due to multiple re-entry circuits. Pharmacological goals are rate control, rhythm control, and prevention of thromboembolic complications. β-blockers (Class II) and Ca²⁺ channel blockers (Class IV) reduce AV nodal conduction and ventricular rate. Digoxin may also be used, particularly in heart failure patients. Rhythm control may involve Class I or III agents such as flecainide or amiodarone, though pro-arrhythmic risks must be considered.

Ventricular dysrhythmias, especially ventricular fibrillation (VF), are immediately life-threatening. Acute VF requires defibrillation rather than pharmacological therapy alone. <font color="#c00000">Lidocaine</font> (Class Ib) is useful for ventricular arrhythmias in ischaemic tissue, while <font color="#c00000">amiodarone</font> is commonly used for broader ventricular rhythm suppression.

Long QT-associated torsades de pointes may be treated with magnesium. Implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) are often essential in recurrent VF prevention.

Strengths of pharmacological therapy include rhythm stabilisation and reduced sudden death risk in selected cases. Limitations include incomplete efficacy, organ toxicity, and potential for drug-induced arrhythmias.

Overall, AF management focuses on haemodynamic optimisation and stroke prevention, whereas ventricular dysrhythmia treatment prioritises immediate survival and suppression of fatal electrical instability. Understanding mechanistic differences is critical for therapeutic success.
