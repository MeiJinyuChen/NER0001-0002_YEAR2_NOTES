## 1.4 α1 Blockade

### A. Consequences of α₁ blockade
- ↓ Blood pressure
- Nasal congestion
	- → vasodilation → ↑ blood volume in mucosa → oedema
- Failure of ejaculation
- Miosis
	- ↓ radial muscle contraction → ↓ pupil dilation → pupil constriction
- Postural hypotension

### B. α₁ blockers and heart failure
- Not first-line antihypertensives
- May ↑ risk of heart failure
- BUT:
	- Cardiac α<sub>₁A</sub> stimulation may be **cardioprotective**
    - Can ↓ preload and afterload (beneficial in HF)
	    - Arteriolar constriction → ↑ total peripheral resistance → ↑ afterload
		- Venoconstriction → ↓ venous capacitance → ↑ venous return → ↑ preload
		
### C. α<sub>1</sub> blockers as BPH drug 
- Prostatic smooth muscle contains α₁<sub>A </sub>subtype
- Drugs:
    - **Tamsulosin / Silodosin (α₁A selective)**  
        → less effect on BP
- Prazosin/doxazosin:
    - Non-selective for α₁ subtypes
    - Used for BP, instead of BPH

# Example Question


## 1. “Compare the pharmacology, therapeutic uses, and adverse effects of α1-adrenoceptor antagonists and β-adrenoceptor antagonists.”

Adrenergic antagonists reduce sympathetic activity either through α- or β-adrenoceptor blockade, producing distinct therapeutic effects. α1-adrenoceptor antagonists such as <font color="#c00000">prazosin, doxazosin, and tamsulosin</font> selectively block vascular and prostatic α1 receptors, preventing noradrenaline-induced vasoconstriction. This causes vasodilation, reduced total peripheral resistance, and lower blood pressure. Tamsulosin is particularly useful in benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) because it selectively blocks α1A receptors in prostatic smooth muscle, improving urinary flow with minimal blood pressure effects. Major limitations include <font color="#c00000">postural hypotension</font>,<font color="#c00000"> reflex tachycardia</font>, <font color="#c00000">nasal congestion</font>, and ejaculatory dysfunction.

β-adrenoceptor antagonists reduce β1-mediated cardiac stimulation and renin release, thereby lowering heart rate, myocardial contractility, and blood pressure. Cardioselective β1 blockers such as <font color="#c00000">atenolol and metoprolol</font> are widely used in <font color="#c00000">hypertension, angina, heart failure</font>, and post-myocardial infarction management. Non-selective agents such as<font color="#c00000"> propranolol</font> also block β2 receptors, which can cause <font color="#c00000">bronchoconstriction</font> and <font color="#c00000">impaired</font> <font color="#c00000">glycogenolysis</font>. This makes non-selective β-blockers contraindicated in <font color="#c00000">asthma</font> and potentially dangerous in <font color="#c00000">diabetes</font> due to masking of hypoglycaemia.

<font color="#c00000">β-blockers have broader cardiovascular benefits than α1 blockers</font>, particularly in reducing mortality after myocardial infarction and improving chronic heart failure outcomes. However, they may cause bradycardia, fatigue, cold extremities, and metabolic disturbances. Vasodilating β-blockers such as <font color="#c00000">carvedilol and nebivolol</font> may offer<font color="#c00000"> improved glycaemic control </font>and <font color="#c00000">cardioprotective effects</font>.

Overall, α1 blockers are particularly valuable for hypertension with coexisting BPH, whereas β-blockers are central to cardiovascular disease management. Drug choice depends on receptor selectivity, patient comorbidities, and side effect profile.


## 2. “Explain the therapeutic uses, strengths, and limitations of β-adrenoceptor antagonists.”

β-adrenoceptor antagonists are among the most important cardiovascular drugs and function primarily through blockade of β1 receptors in the heart and kidney. By reducing cAMP production, β1 blockade lowers intracellular Ca²⁺, decreasing heart rate, myocardial contractility, and renin release. This reduces cardiac output and blood pressure, making β-blockers highly effective in hypertension, angina, arrhythmias, myocardial infarction, and chronic heart failure.

Examples include atenolol and metoprolol (β1 selective), propranolol (non-selective), and carvedilol (mixed α/β blocker). <font color="#c00000">Compared to α-blockers, β-blockers reduce myocardial oxygen demand, prolong diastole, and improve survival following myocardial infarction.</font> In heart failure, agents such as <font color="#c00000">carvedilol can reduce pathological sympathetic remodelling and improve long-term outcomes.</font>

Non-cardiovascular uses include anxiety, migraine prophylaxis, thyrotoxicosis, and glaucoma. <font color="#c00000">Propranolol is particularly effective for performance anxiety</font> due to CNS penetration.

Strengths include broad therapeutic utility, mortality benefit in major cardiovascular diseases, and strong mechanistic rationale. However, limitations arise from side effects and contraindications. Common adverse effects include <font color="#c00000">bradycardia, fatigue, cold extremities, depression, and sleep disturbances</font>. β2 blockade can cause bronchoconstriction, making these drugs unsuitable for asthma patients. In diabetics, β-blockers may worsen glycaemic control, increase insulin resistance, and mask hypoglycaemia symptoms.

Drugs with intrinsic sympathomimetic activity (e.g., pindolol) may reduce resting bradycardia but are less protective after myocardial infarction. Vasodilating β-blockers such as nebivolol may improve metabolic outcomes.

In summary, β-blockers remain foundational in modern therapeutics due to their effectiveness and survival benefits, though careful patient selection is essential.


## 3. “Describe pharmacological strategies that inhibit adrenergic neurotransmission pre-synaptically.”

Adrenergic neurotransmission can be inhibited pre-synaptically by targeting noradrenaline (NA) synthesis, storage, or release. These approaches reduce sympathetic output and lower blood pressure but often have significant systemic limitations.

Synthesis inhibitors act by blocking catecholamine biosynthesis enzymes. <font color="#c00000">α-Methyltyrosine</font> inhibits tyrosine hydroxylase, the rate-limiting step converting tyrosine to L-DOPA, thereby reducing all downstream catecholamine synthesis. <font color="#c00000">α-Methyldopa</font> is metabolised to α-methyl noradrenaline, a false transmitter that selectively activates presynaptic α2 receptors, suppressing NA release and reducing sympathetic tone. This mechanism has been historically important in hypertension management.

Storage inhibitors such as <font color="#c00000">reserpine</font> block VMAT (vesicular monoamine transporter), preventing NA storage in synaptic vesicles. Cytoplasmic NA is subsequently degraded by MAO, causing long-term depletion of sympathetic neurotransmitter stores. Although effective in lowering blood pressure, reserpine has severe CNS side effects, including depression, due to monoamine depletion.

Release inhibitors such as <font color="#c00000">guanethidine</font> enter nerve terminals via NET, accumulate in vesicles, and gradually replace NA. This depletes functional transmitter stores and may also inhibit action potential propagation. Guanethidine produces powerful antihypertensive effects but commonly causes severe orthostatic hypotension.

These strategies provide strong suppression of adrenergic function but are limited by poor selectivity and widespread adverse effects. Modern therapies generally favour receptor-selective antagonists instead.

Overall, pre-synaptic adrenergic inhibition demonstrates fundamental pharmacological principles but is less commonly used clinically today due to toxicity and better alternatives.
