
# 1. Definition & Mechanism

## 1. Definition

- Produce reversible regional loss of sensation (pain) without loss of consciousness

## 1.2 Mechanism of Action 

- Target: Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels (Na<sub>v</sub>)
- Action:
    - Bind to **inner pore of Na⁺ channel (S6 segments, especially IVS6)**
    - Stabilise **inactivated state → prevent Na⁺ influx → block AP generation & conduction**
    
### pH-dependent Mechanism

- Most are weak bases
	- Administered as water-soluble hydrochlorides (B·HCl)
	- B = tertiary amine base
	- B is liberated by the relatively alkaline pH of tissue fluids

- Key concept:
    - ↓ pH (infection) → ↓ non-ionised form → ↓ efficacy

# 2. Properties

## 2.1 Physiological Properties

### A. **Use-Dependence 

- Due to 2 different pathways
- Definition:
	- Block develops faster and is greater when the fibre is:
			1. High-frequency firing (e.g. 10Hz vs. 1Hz)
			2. Longer AP duration
- Preferentially block pain fibres (Aδ, C) during noxious stimulation than Aα motor fibres 
	- Due to high-frequency firing

- Hydrophilic pathway:
	- Drug enters the channel from the inside of the cell (cytoplasm)
- Hydrophobic pathway:
	- Drug reaches binding site directly through the lipid membrane

| Feature            | Hydrophilic        | Hydrophobic                |
| ------------------ | ------------------ | -------------------------- |
| Drug form          | BH⁺                | B                          |
| Entry              | Inside cell        | Through membrane           |
| Channel state      | inactivated + open | inactivated + open + close |
| **Use-dependence** | **Strong**         | **Weak**                   |

### B. Differential Action

- Blocked more readily:
	- Myelinated > Unmyelinated
	- Small diameter > Large diameter

## 2.2 **Pharmacological Properties**

### A. **Potency**

- Determined by **lipid solubility**
- ↑ lipophilicity → ↑ potency

### B. **Onset**

- Determined by **pKa**
- Lower pKa → more non-ionised drug → **faster onset**

### C. **Duration**

- Determined by ability of binding to plasma & tissue proteins
- ↑ binding → ↑ duration

## 2.4 Side Effects

### Situation

- May occur with infiltration and spinal anaesthesia

### CNS effects

- Low concentrations
	- Tinnitus
	- Blurring of vision
	- Drowsiness
	- Numbness of tongue
- High concentrations
	- Agitation
	- Convulsions (occasionally)
		- → profound CNS depression + respiratory depression

### Cardiovascular effects

- Hypotension
	- vasodilation → depression of myocardium → cardiac slowing
- Cardiac block

# Example Question

## 1. “Describe the mechanism of action of local anaesthetics and explain how pH and use-dependence influence their clinical effects.”

Local anaesthetics produce reversible regional loss of sensation by blocking voltage-gated Na⁺ channels (Nav) in peripheral nerves, thereby preventing action potential generation and conduction. Most local anaesthetics are <font color="#c00000">weak bases</font> administered as <font color="#c00000">water-soluble hydrochloride salts</font>. In tissue fluids, they exist in equilibrium between non-ionised (B) and ionised (BH⁺) forms. The non-ionised form diffuses across neuronal membranes, while the ionised form binds to the intracellular portion of the Na⁺ channel, particularly within the S6 segment, stabilising the channel’s inactivated state and blocking Na⁺ influx.

This mechanism is strongly influenced by tissue pH. In acidic environments such as infected tissue, a larger proportion of drug becomes ionised extracellularly, reducing membrane penetration and decreasing anaesthetic efficacy. This explains why local anaesthetics are less effective in inflamed tissues.

Local anaesthetics also exhibit use-dependence, meaning blockade is greater in nerves with frequent firing or prolonged depolarisation. Rapidly firing pain fibres (Aδ and C fibres) are therefore preferentially blocked over less active motor fibres. This occurs because repeated channel opening increases access of the drug to its binding site, especially through the hydrophilic intracellular pathway.

Clinically, this provides selective pain relief while preserving motor function initially. Use-dependence also underlies antiarrhythmic uses of certain Na⁺ channel blockers such as lidocaine in cardiac tissue.

Thus, local anaesthetic efficacy depends not only on receptor binding but also on ionisation state and physiological activity of the target nerve. These principles are central to understanding both therapeutic success and limitations.

## 2. “Compare ester and amide local anaesthetics, including pharmacokinetics, advantages, and limitations.”

Local anaesthetics are structurally divided into ester-linked and amide-linked compounds, which differ significantly in metabolism, duration, stability, and toxicity.

Ester local anaesthetics, such as <font color="#c00000">procaine and tetracaine</font>, are metabolised rapidly by plasma and tissue esterases, including butyrylcholinesterase. Because of rapid hydrolysis, they generally have shorter duration of action and are less stable in solution. A major disadvantage is production of para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA) metabolites, which increase the risk of allergic reactions. For this reason, ester anaesthetics are less commonly used today.

Amide local anaesthetics, such as <font color="#c00000">lidocaine and prilocaine</font>, are metabolised primarily by hepatic amidases. They are more chemically stable, longer acting, and generally produce fewer allergic reactions. Their greater stability and predictable pharmacokinetics make them the preferred agents for most clinical applications, including infiltration, nerve block, epidural, and topical anaesthesia.

Pharmacological properties also vary with lipid solubility, pKa, and protein binding. <font color="#c00000">Increased lipid solubility enhances potency, lower pKa produces faster onset, and stronger protein binding increases duration.</font>

Although amides are generally safer, <font color="#c00000">hepatic impairment</font> may reduce clearance and increase toxicity risk. Systemic toxicity for both classes can affect CNS and cardiovascular systems, causing <font color="#c00000">seizures, hypotension, arrhythmias, and respiratory depression</font>.

Overall, amide local anaesthetics offer superior stability, longer duration, and lower allergenicity, making them more clinically useful than ester agents. However, patient liver function and toxicity risk remain important therapeutic considerations.

## 3. “Discuss the therapeutic applications, strengths, and limitations of local anaesthetic administration methods.”

Local anaesthetics can be administered through multiple routes depending on desired anatomical specificity, duration, and procedure type. Common methods include topical, infiltration, nerve block, spinal, epidural, and intravenous regional anaesthesia.

Topical anaesthesia, using agents such as <font color="#c00000">lidocaine or benzocaine</font>, is applied directly to mucosal surfaces and is useful for<font color="#c00000"> minor procedures</font> involving the mouth, cornea, urethra, or airway. It is simple and minimally invasive but limited to superficial tissues.

Infiltration anaesthesia involves direct injection into tissue, commonly for <font color="#c00000">wound closure, episiotomy, vasectomy, or minor surgery</font>. Lidocaine is widely used, often combined with <font color="#c00000">vasoconstrictors</font> such as <font color="#c00000">adrenaline</font> to delay systemic absorption, prolong action, and reduce toxicity.

Nerve blocks provide regional anaesthesia by <font color="#c00000">targeting major nerve trunks</font>, such as<font color="#c00000"> mandibular nerve block in dentistry or brachial plexus block in upper limb surgery</font>. These provide precise regional control but require technical expertise.

Spinal anaesthesia involves injection into the subarachnoid space, producing profound lower-body anaesthesia suitable for <font color="#c00000">caesarean sections or major surgery</font>. Epidural anaesthesia is delivered outside the dura and is particularly valuable in <font color="#c00000">labour analgesia</font> due to controllable duration.

Each method has strengths in specificity and efficacy but carries risks including systemic toxicity, hypotension, CNS effects, or nerve injury. Vasoconstrictor use may improve efficacy but risks <font color="#c00000">local ischaemia</font>.

Overall, route selection depends on balancing procedural requirements, patient safety, and drug pharmacology. Understanding these methods is essential for therapeutic optimisation and minimising complications.
