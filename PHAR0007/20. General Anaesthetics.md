

# 1. Definition of General Anaesthesia

## Definition
- **Drug-induced reversible loss of consciousness**
- Produces:
    - Unconsciousness
    - Amnesia
    - Analgesia
    - Immobility
    - Often muscle relaxation

## Clinical Measurement

| Measure                  | Definition                                                                | Importance                  |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| **LORR**                 | Loss of righting reflex                                                   | Experimental proxy          |
| **Unresponsiveness**     | No response to verbal command                                             | Clinical                    |
| **Surgical anaesthesia** | No response to painful stimulus                                           | True operative endpoint     |
| **ED50/EC50**            | Effective dose in 50% patients                                            | Potency                     |
| **MAC**                  | Minimum alveolar concentration producing no pain response in 50% patients | Standard for inhaled agents |
- Lower MAC = more potent
- Example:
    - **Halothane:** MAC ~0.7% (high potency)
    - **Nitrous oxide:** MAC ~101% (weak; cannot be sole surgical anaesthetic)

# 2. Classification of General Anaesthetics

## A. Inhalational Agents
### Features
- Absorbed through lungs
- Gases or volatile liquids
### Drugs
- <font color="#c00000">Isoflurane</font>
- Enflurane
- <font color="#c00000">Halothane</font>
### Clinical Use
- Often combined with <font color="#c00000">NO and xenon</font>
### Mechanism
- Targets much less clear

  Cys-loop receptors (inc. GABA<sub>A</sub>Rs)

### Drugs
- <font color="#c00000">Etomidate</font>

# 4. Pharmacokinetics of General Anaesthetics

## 4.1 Inhaled Agents
### Speed determined by:
- Blood/gas partition coefficient (λ)
- Inspired concentration
- Ventilation rate
- Tissue perfusion
### Rule:
- **Low blood solubility → faster induction/recovery**
- Example:
    - N₂O (fast)
    - Halothane (slow)
## 4.2 Intravenous Agents

### Thiopentone:
- Rapid CNS uptake
- Short action due to redistribution to muscle/fat
- Not metabolism initially
### Propofol:
- Rapid hepatic metabolism
- Suitable for TIVA (total intravenous anaesthesia)
- Minimal accumulation

# Example Question


## 1. “Compare inhalational and intravenous general anaesthetics in terms of mechanism, pharmacokinetics, and clinical application.”

General anaesthetics produce reversible loss of consciousness, amnesia, analgesia, and immobility, enabling surgical procedures. They are broadly divided into inhalational and intravenous agents.

Inhalational anaesthetics such as isoflurane, halothane, nitrous oxide, and xenon are delivered via the lungs. Their <font color="#c00000">potency</font> is measured by <font color="#c00000">minimum alveolar concentration (MAC)</font>, with lower MAC indicating higher potency. Their <font color="#c00000">speed of induction and recovery </font>is determined primarily by<font color="#c00000"> blood/gas partition coefficient</font>: low blood solubility allows faster equilibration and recovery. <font color="#c00000">Nitrous oxide acts rapidly but is weak, whereas halothane is more potent but slower</font>. Mechanistically, inhaled agents have complex actions involving GABA_A receptor potentiation, K⁺ channel modulation, and NMDA inhibition.

Intravenous agents such as propofol, thiopentone, and etomidate generally act through positive allosteric modulation of GABA_A receptors. Propofol is particularly important due to rapid onset, rapid metabolism, anti-emetic properties, and suitability for total intravenous anaesthesia (TIVA). Thiopentone acts rapidly but is terminated mainly by redistribution rather than metabolism. Ketamine is distinct, acting primarily as an NMDA antagonist.

IV agents are preferred for induction due to rapid CNS delivery, while inhaled agents are often used for maintenance because of adjustable depth. Propofol offers rapid recovery but may cause hypotension and respiratory depression, whereas inhaled agents allow continuous titration but may irritate airways or trigger malignant hyperthermia.

Overall, clinical choice depends on balancing speed, safety, depth control, and patient physiology.


## 2. “Describe the pharmacology, strengths, and limitations of propofol and ketamine.”

Propofol and ketamine are major intravenous general anaesthetics with distinct pharmacological profiles.

Propofol is a GABA_A receptor positive allosteric modulator that enhances inhibitory neurotransmission, producing rapid unconsciousness. It is highly lipophilic, allowing fast CNS penetration, and is rapidly metabolised hepatically, resulting in quick recovery and minimal accumulation. Propofol is widely used for induction, maintenance, and procedural sedation, particularly in TIVA.

Its strengths include:
- Rapid onset
- Rapid recovery
- Anti-emetic effects
- Smooth induction
- Low postoperative confusion

Limitations include:
- <font color="#c00000">Hypotension</font>
- <font color="#c00000">Respiratory depression</font>
- Pain on injection
- No intrinsic analgesia

Ketamine differs fundamentally by acting primarily as an NMDA receptor antagonist. It produces dissociative anaesthesia, preserving airway reflexes and often stimulating cardiovascular activity through sympathetic activation. This makes ketamine particularly useful in trauma, haemodynamic instability, or paediatric anaesthesia.

Its strengths include:
- Strong analgesia
- Maintained airway reflexes
- Less respiratory depression
- Cardiovascular support

Limitations include:
- <font color="#c00000">Hallucinations</font>
- Emergence delirium
- Increased intracranial pressure
- <font color="#c00000">Abuse liability</font>

Thus, propofol is often preferred for controlled surgical anaesthesia, while ketamine is especially valuable when haemodynamic preservation or analgesia is prioritised.



## 3. “Compare general and local anaesthetics, including mechanisms, therapeutic uses, and limitations.”

General and local anaesthetics both facilitate medical procedures but differ fundamentally in mechanism, target site, and physiological effect.

General anaesthetics act primarily within the CNS to produce unconsciousness, amnesia, analgesia, and immobility. Most intravenous agents such as propofol act through GABA<sub>A</sub> receptor potentiation, while ketamine primarily blocks NMDA receptors. Inhaled agents additionally modulate ion channels and neurotransmitter systems. General anaesthesia is essential for major surgery but carries risks including respiratory depression, cardiovascular suppression, postoperative nausea, and malignant hyperthermia.

Local anaesthetics such as lidocaine and prilocaine act peripherally by blocking voltage-gated Na⁺ channels, preventing action potential conduction in nerves. They produce reversible regional sensory loss without loss of consciousness. Their efficacy depends on pH, pKa, and use-dependence, allowing selective pain fibre blockade.

Strengths of local anaesthesia include:
- Preserved consciousness
- Reduced systemic CNS depression
- Targeted analgesia
- Lower overall physiological disruption

Limitations include:
- Incomplete coverage for major surgery
- Reduced efficacy in acidic tissue
- CNS/cardiovascular toxicity if systemically absorbed

Clinically, local anaesthesia is ideal for minor or regional procedures, whereas general anaesthesia is necessary for extensive surgery requiring unconsciousness.

Overall, therapeutic choice depends on balancing procedural demands, systemic safety, and pharmacological properties.
