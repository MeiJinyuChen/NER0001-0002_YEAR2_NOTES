## 4 structurally distinct families
1. Ligand-gated ion channels (LGICs)
2. GPCRs
3. Enzyme-linked receptors
4. Nuclear receptors

## 1. LGICs #LGICs

### - Structure
- Multi-subunit transmembrane proteins that contain an symmetrical integral ion channel
- LGICs combine the functions of receptor and effector into a single macromolecule
- Some only form homomers, some only heteromers and some can form either → Huge diversity (e.g. in different body regions for different functions)
- Each subtype can be specifically targetd. e.g. Tubocurarinne as a poison to block AChR in the neuromuscular junctions

### - nAChR #nAChR
- 17 nAChR subunits have been identified
	- α subunit – 10 isoforms 
	- β subunit – 4 isoforms 
	- γ subunit
	- δ subunit
	- ε subunit 
- Gives rise to many flavours of nAChRs, for example:
	- Muscle: (α1)2β1δε
	- Ganglion type: (α3)2(β4)3
	- CNS (hetero): (α4)2(β2)3

## - Mechanism
- When ion channels open, they shift the local membrane potential towards their reversal potential 
- Non-selective cation channels (Na<sup>+</sup>, K<sup>+</sup>, Ca<sup>2+</sup>) like iGluRs or nAChRs reverse around 0 mV
- Chloride selective channels like GABA<sub>A</sub>Rs or GlyRs reverse around –70 mV![[capture_20251129155342669.bmp|450]]

## 2. GPCRs #GPCRs 

- The most common type of plasma membrane receptor
- Amongst the oldest of signal transduction mechanisms
- Make up ~4% of protein-coding genes 
- Are the targets for around 50% of current therapeutic drugs
### -Structure
- = 7 TM α-helices. Single peptide chain (although some must dimerize to be functional). Extracellular N-terminus, intracellular C-terminus.
- Agonists normally bind at either a specialized N-terminal ligand binding region e.g. mGluRs, or within the membrane helices e.g. mAChRs.
- Several GPCRs exist as dimers (either homo-dimers or hetero-dimers). e.g. GABA<sub>B1</sub> and GABA<sub>B2</sub> only function when assembled as a dimer with the other GABA<sub>B</sub> subunit.
<font color="#00b050">5 different families with no obvious sequence homology</font>

### - Mechanism 
- The α subunit binds GDP and has a GTPase activity (catalysing the conversion of GTP to GDP). The β and γ subunits associate tightly into a dimer and can anchor themselves into the plasma membrane through a lipid modification (prenylation) of the γ subunit. <font color="#00b050">Prenylation: The covalent attachment of a lipid group to a protein, usually at the cysteine near the protein's C-terminus.</font>
- The receptor acts as a GEF (GTP/GDP exchange factor)
1. Ligand binding lead to a conformational change in receptor, allowing the α subunit to interact with the receptor (via the 3rd intracellular loop).
2. Binding of the α subunit to the activated receptor triggers exchange of GDP for GTP.
3. The activated G-protein dissociates from the receptor and separates into α and βγ subunits.![[capture_20251129160820967.bmp|325]]
4. The GTP bound α subunit interacts with the effector protein AC (Adenylate cyclase), leading to activation of AC and the conversion of ATP to cyclic AMP (cAMP).
5. cAMP acts as a second messenger, which bind to and activate PKA. PKA phosphorylates many different target proteins.
6. The signal is terminated by a GTPase function of the α subunit which hydrolyzes GTP to GDP. Hydrolysis of GTP leads to dissociation of the α subunit from AC which is therefore inactivated.
– This hydrolysis may be accelerated by other proteins that act as GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs), specific for Gα subunits, e.g. Regulators of G-proteins (RGS). In some cases, the effector itself possess intrinsic GAP activity, e.g. PLCβ.
7. Finally, the α and βγ subunits re-associate and the response is terminated ready for the next activation.

### - RTKs
- There are 58 RTKs subdivided into 20 families.
- Most are single subunit receptors but some exist as complexes, e.g., the insulin receptor.

- Responses operate on the<span style="background:#fff88f"> minutes/hours time scale.</span>
#### - Mechanism
Example: Platelet derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR)
1. PDGF Binds to a receptor. 
2. Receptors dimerize.
3. A conformational change allows ATP binding. 
4. Then autophosphorylation of tyrosine residues. 
5. Phosphorylated tyrosine residues act as recognition sites for effector proteins with src homology SH2, SH3 domains, and can dock them.
6. SH2 binding causes changes in localization, enzyme activity, complex assembly transcriptional control.

## 4. Nuclear receptors #Nuclear_receptors
= Intracellular DNA binding receptors (can be in the cytoplasm not just nucleus).

Members: Steroid hormone receptors
Example agonists: Oestradiol
### - Structure
- Soluble.
- Their ligands are membrane permeable (e.g. steroid hormones).
- The receptors have ligand binding, DNA binding and transcription activating domains.
![[capture_20251129170550383.bmp]]

### - Mechanism
1. Through gene transcription.
2. Typically very slow time-course for steroid action. Very commonly trigger epigenetic changes (real modification), apart from temporarily activating a gene. The effect can be life or years long.

- Binding of ligand (e.g. oestrogen) promotes the formation of receptor dimers. The dimeric, ligand-bound receptor is able to act as a transcription factor by binding to specific regions of DNA (hormone-responsive elements).

# Example Question


## 1. “Compare ligand-gated ion channels and G-protein coupled receptors in terms of structure, signalling mechanisms, therapeutic relevance, and pharmacological importance.”

Ligand-gated ion channels (LGICs) and G-protein coupled receptors (GPCRs) are two major plasma membrane receptor families that differ significantly in structure, signalling speed, and therapeutic application.

LGICs are multi-subunit transmembrane proteins that combine receptor and ion channel functions within a single macromolecule. Upon ligand binding, the channel opens directly, allowing rapid ion flux across membranes. This produces extremely fast responses, typically within milliseconds. Examples include nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs), GABA_A receptors, and NMDA receptors. LGICs are critical in fast synaptic transmission, muscle contraction, and inhibitory CNS signalling. Drugs targeting LGICs include benzodiazepines (GABA_A) and tubocurarine (nAChR antagonist).

GPCRs are structurally distinct seven-transmembrane receptors that activate intracellular G proteins rather than directly gating channels. Upon agonist binding, GDP-GTP exchange activates Gα subunits, which regulate effectors such as adenylate cyclase or phospholipase C. GPCR signalling is slower (seconds to minutes) but highly amplified through second messengers such as cAMP, IP₃, and DAG. Major pathways include Gs, Gi, and Gq systems.

GPCRs are therapeutically more versatile and represent targets for approximately half of current drugs, including β-blockers, opioids, adrenergic agonists, and muscarinic antagonists.

Thus, LGICs provide rapid direct signalling, while GPCRs offer slower but amplified and modulatable signalling. Pharmacologically, GPCRs dominate therapeutic targeting due to greater regulatory complexity and tissue diversity.



## 2. “Describe the major receptor families involved in pharmacology and explain how receptor structure determines signalling timescale and therapeutic application.”

Pharmacology involves four major receptor families: ligand-gated ion channels (LGICs), G-protein coupled receptors (GPCRs), enzyme-linked receptors, and nuclear receptors.

LGICs are membrane ion channels activated directly by ligand binding, producing rapid responses within milliseconds. They mediate fast neurotransmission and are targeted by drugs such as benzodiazepines and neuromuscular blockers.

GPCRs are seven-transmembrane receptors that activate intracellular G proteins, generating second messengers such as cAMP or IP₃. Their signalling occurs over seconds to minutes and is highly amplified. GPCRs are the most common therapeutic targets, including β-adrenoceptors, muscarinic receptors, and opioid receptors.

Enzyme-linked receptors, such as receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs), are activated by peptide hormones and growth factors like insulin or EGF. Ligand binding induces receptor dimerisation and phosphorylation cascades, producing responses over minutes to hours. These receptors are highly important in metabolism, growth, and oncology.

Nuclear receptors are intracellular DNA-binding proteins activated by lipophilic ligands such as steroid hormones. They directly regulate gene transcription and have very slow but prolonged effects, often lasting hours to years. Steroid therapeutics and selective estrogen receptor modulators act through this system.

Receptor structure fundamentally determines signalling speed, amplification, and therapeutic use. Fast membrane ion channels support acute signalling, whereas intracellular transcriptional receptors regulate long-term physiology.

Overall, understanding receptor families is essential for predicting drug effects, therapeutic windows, and side effect profiles.
