# 2. Heart Failure

= Reduced cardiac output

## 2.1 Causes → Symptoms

- ↓ CO
- Neurohumoral compensation → initially helpful → ultimately worsens HF

### **Neurohumoral compensation**

<img src="assets/Pasted%20image%2020260423144238.png" width="450" alt="Pasted image 20260423144238">

- ↓ CO → ↓ BP → ↑ sympathetic output + RAAS activation
- ↑ sympathetic output
	1.  **→ Arterial constriction (↑ afterload)**
		- → ↑ CO → Harder for heart to pump out
		- → ↑ HR (to compensate)
	2.  **→ Venous constriction (↑ preload)**
		- → Oedema
- RAAS activation →
	- → renin release → Angiotensin II 
	1. → Arterial  constriction (↑ afterload) 
		- → ↑ HR
    2. → Aldosterone → Na⁺/H₂O retention → ↑ Blood volume → ↑ Preload 
	    - → Oedema

### Symptoms

- Tachycardia
- Cardiac remodelling
- Shortness of breath (dyspnoea)
- Oedema

## **2.2 Therapeutic Strategy**

<img src="assets/Pasted%20image%2020260423154446.png" alt="Pasted image 20260423154446">

**RAAS system**

<img src="assets/Pasted%20image%2020260423155235.png" alt="Pasted image 20260423155235">

### Aims 

 - Slow/Reduce cardiac remodelling 
- → Improve symptoms
- → Reduce hospitalization
- → Reduce mortality

### Strategies

1. Direct (= ionotropes)
	- Mechanism: ↑ Cardiac contractions
	- Short-term
2. Indirect
	- Mechanism: ↓ Work load
		- ↓ Arterial pressure → ↓ After load
		- ↓ Venous pressure → ↓ Preload
	- Long-term

### <font color="#c00000">Gold standard treatment</font>

- RAAS inhibitors (ACE inhibitor / Ang II R antagonist)
- Spironolactone
- Carvedilol

### Limitation

- No treatment is effective in HF with preserved ejection fraciton

# Example Question

## 1. “Describe the pathophysiology of heart failure and explain the pharmacological rationale for current therapeutic strategies.”

Heart failure is characterised by reduced cardiac output, most commonly due to left ventricular dysfunction. As cardiac output falls, blood pressure decreases, triggering compensatory activation of the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS).

Initially, sympathetic stimulation increases heart rate and contractility, while arterial and venous constriction help maintain perfusion. However, chronic SNS activation increases <font color="#c00000">afterload, myocardial oxygen demand, tachycardia, and arrhythmogenic risk</font>. Venous constriction raises preload and contributes to oedema.

RAAS activation worsens this process. Angiotensin II causes vasoconstriction, increasing afterload, while aldosterone promotes Na⁺ and water retention, increasing preload and pulmonary congestion. <font color="#c00000">Long-term, these systems drive maladaptive cardiac remodelling, fibrosis, and disease progression</font>.

Modern therapy therefore focuses less on direct inotropy and more on reducing pathological compensation. ACE inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) reduce afterload and remodelling while improving survival. β-blockers reduce sympathetic overactivation, lower heart rate, and improve long-term mortality despite possible short-term deterioration. Mineralocorticoid antagonists such as spironolactone further improve survival by reducing fluid retention and remodelling.

<font color="#c00000">Diuretics rapidly relieve symptoms by reducing preload and pulmonary oedema but offer limited mortality benefit</font>. Digoxin improves symptoms through increased contractility and AV nodal slowing but has a narrow therapeutic index and no survival advantage.

Overall, effective heart failure management targets neurohumoral maladaptation rather than solely boosting contractility, making RAAS inhibitors, β-blockers, and mineralocorticoid antagonists the therapeutic foundation.

## 2. “Compare the pharmacology, strengths, and limitations of digoxin and β-blockers in cardiovascular disease.”

Digoxin and β-blockers are important cardiovascular drugs but differ substantially in mechanism, therapeutic role, and long-term benefit.

Digoxin is a cardiac glycoside that inhibits Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase in cardiac myocytes. This increases intracellular Na⁺, reduces Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger activity, and raises intracellular Ca²⁺, thereby<font color="#c00000"> increasing contractility </font>(positive inotropy). <font color="#c00000">Digoxin also enhances vagal tone, slowing AV conduction and increasing refractory period, which is useful in atrial fibrillation.</font>

Its main strength is symptomatic improvement in heart failure and ventricular rate control in AF. However, digoxin has a <font color="#c00000">narrow therapeutic index</font><font color="#c00000">, no proven mortality benefit, and significant risks including arrhythmias, nausea</font>, and toxicity exacerbated by electrolyte imbalance.

β-blockers such as carvedilol, bisoprolol, and atenolol reduce β1-mediated sympathetic stimulation, <font color="#c00000">decreasing heart rate, myocardial contractility, renin release, and oxygen demand</font>. In heart failure, hypertension, and angina, β-blockers reduce pathological neurohumoral stress, improve myocardial efficiency, and<font color="#c00000"> significantly increase survival</font>.

Their strengths include mortality reduction, broad cardiovascular utility, and anti-remodelling effects. Limitations include bradycardia, fatigue, bronchospasm (particularly non-selective agents), and potential early worsening in heart failure.

Thus, digoxin primarily offers symptomatic and rhythm benefits, whereas β-blockers provide major long-term prognostic improvement. In modern therapeutics, β-blockers are foundational, while digoxin serves as adjunctive therapy in selected patients.

## 3. “Discuss pharmacological strategies for treating hypertension and angina.”

Hypertension and angina are major cardiovascular disorders with distinct pathophysiology but overlapping pharmacological strategies.

Hypertension primarily involves elevated total peripheral resistance (TPR), requiring therapies that reduce vascular tone, blood volume, or cardiac output. <font color="#c00000">Diuretics </font>reduce plasma volume and preload,<font color="#c00000"> α1 blockers</font> induce vasodilation, <font color="#c00000">Ca²⁺ channel blockers</font> reduce arterial smooth muscle contraction, <font color="#c00000">β-blockers </font>reduce cardiac output and renin release, and <font color="#c00000">ACE inhibitors </font>suppress RAAS-mediated vasoconstriction. Drug choice depends on comorbidities, tolerability, and long-term cardiovascular risk reduction.

Angina occurs when myocardial oxygen demand exceeds supply, usually due to coronary artery stenosis or vasospasm. Treatment aims to reduce oxygen demand or improve coronary perfusion. <font color="#c00000">β-blockers</font> lower heart rate and contractility, prolonging diastole and reducing demand. <font color="#c00000">Nitrates</font> donate nitric oxide, increasing cGMP and causing venodilation, which reduces preload and myocardial work while improving coronary blood flow.<font color="#c00000"> Ca²⁺ channel blockers</font> reduce afterload and coronary vasospasm.

β-blockers are particularly important because they benefit both hypertension and angina while improving long-term outcomes. Nitrates are highly effective for acute symptom relief but limited by tolerance and headache. ACE inhibitors provide strong cardiovascular protection in hypertensive patients with heart failure or vascular disease.

Overall, cardiovascular pharmacology emphasises mechanism-driven reduction of workload, vascular resistance, and neurohumoral stress. Rational combination therapy often provides optimal disease control while minimising toxicity.
