
# 1. Definition and Biological Importance of Pain

## 1.1 Definition:

- Pain = unpleasant sensory + emotional experience associated with actual or potential tissue damage
- Protective in acute settings
- Chronic pain = maladaptive pathological state

## 1.2 Classification of Pain

|Type|Mechanism|Examples|Therapeutic implications|
|---|---|---|---|
|**Nociceptive**|Activation of nociceptors by tissue injury/inflammation|Arthritis, acute injury|NSAIDs, opioids|
|**Neuropathic**|Lesion/disease of somatosensory system|Diabetic neuropathy, trigeminal neuralgia|Gabapentin, TCAs|
|**Nociplastic**|Altered nociception without tissue damage|Fibromyalgia|Difficult treatment|

# 2. Pain Pathway

## 2.1 Sequential stages

### A. Reception (Transduction)

- Occurs in nociceptors

### B. Transmission

- Aδ fibres → fast sharp pain
- C fibres → slow dull pain

### C. Integration (Spinal Cord)

- Dorsal horn
- First synapse
- Wind-up can occur here
- Major NTs:
    - Glutamate
    - Substance P
    - CGRP

### D. Perception (Brain)

- Thalamus
- Somatosensory cortex
- Limbic system
- Prefrontal cortex

#### Pain includes:

- Sensory component
- Emotional suffering
- Cognitive interpretation

### E. Sensitisation

- Involved in chronic/neuropathic pain
- Increase in neuronal firing under long-lasting stimulus
- Only in C fibres. Not in Aδ fibres.
- LTP-like process
  NMDARs-dependent

# Example Question

## 1. “Describe the pain pathway and explain how different analgesic drugs target distinct stages of pain processing.”

Pain is a complex sensory and emotional experience involving multiple sequential stages: transduction, transmission, spinal integration, perception, and sensitisation.

Transduction occurs in peripheral nociceptors, where tissue injury activates Aδ and C fibres through inflammatory mediators such as prostaglandins, bradykinin, and cytokines. NSAIDs such as ibuprofen reduce this stage by inhibiting COX enzymes and prostaglandin synthesis.

Transmission involves action potential conduction to the dorsal horn via voltage-gated Na⁺ channels. Local anaesthetics such as lidocaine block Nav channels, preventing signal propagation.

At the dorsal horn, neurotransmitters including glutamate, substance P, and CGRP mediate synaptic transmission. Gabapentinoids reduce neurotransmitter release by inhibiting voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels.

Repeated C-fibre stimulation can induce NMDA-dependent wind-up and central sensitisation, contributing to chronic pain. Ketamine reduces this process through NMDA receptor antagonism.

Descending inhibitory pathways involving serotonin and noradrenaline suppress pain transmission. TCAs and SNRIs enhance these systems and are especially useful in neuropathic pain.

Opioids act broadly at central and peripheral levels via μ-opioid receptors, reducing neurotransmitter release and hyperpolarising neurons.

Thus, pain pharmacology is highly stage-specific. Effective treatment depends on matching drug mechanism to pain type and pathophysiological stage.

## 2. “Compare NSAIDs and opioids as analgesics, including mechanisms, therapeutic strengths, and limitations.”

NSAIDs and opioids are major analgesic classes but differ fundamentally in mechanism and clinical role.

NSAIDs such as ibuprofen and aspirin inhibit cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes, reducing prostaglandin synthesis and thereby decreasing peripheral nociceptor sensitisation. They are particularly effective in inflammatory pain states such as arthritis or acute injury.

Strengths include:

- Good peripheral efficacy
- Anti-inflammatory action
- No addiction risk
- Useful first-line agents

Limitations include:
- GI bleeding
- Renal toxicity
- Cardiovascular risk
- Limited efficacy in severe or neuropathic pain

Opioids such as morphine, fentanyl, and oxycodone act primarily through μ-opioid receptors (Gi/o-coupled), reducing Ca²⁺ influx, increasing K⁺ efflux, suppressing neurotransmitter release, and hyperpolarising pain pathways.

Strengths include:
- Most powerful acute analgesia
- Broad efficacy
- Severe pain management

Limitations include:
- Respiratory depression
- Sedation
- Constipation
- Tolerance
- Dependence
- Addiction
- Hyperalgesia with chronic use

Thus, NSAIDs are preferred for mild-to-moderate inflammatory pain, whereas opioids are reserved for severe acute pain due to greater efficacy but substantial safety concerns.

## 3. “Discuss pharmacological strategies for treating neuropathic pain.”

Neuropathic pain arises from lesions or dysfunction of the somatosensory nervous system and differs significantly from nociceptive pain in mechanism and treatment response.

Peripheral ectopic firing can be reduced by Na⁺ channel blockers such as carbamazepine, which is particularly effective in trigeminal neuralgia.

Gabapentin and pregabalin inhibit voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channel activity, reducing glutamate release and central excitability. These are common first-line agents.

Descending inhibitory systems are enhanced by TCAs and SNRIs, which increase serotonin and noradrenaline signalling, improving chronic pain control.

Topical lidocaine and capsaicin may reduce peripheral sensitisation in selected patients.

Central sensitisation plays a major role in refractory neuropathic pain. Ketamine, as an NMDA antagonist, can reduce wind-up and severe chronic pain states.

Traditional NSAIDs are generally less effective because inflammation is often not the primary driver. Opioids may be used in refractory cases but are limited by dependence risk.

Overall, neuropathic pain treatment requires mechanism-specific targeting of aberrant neuronal excitability, central sensitisation, and descending modulation rather than simple peripheral anti-inflammatory therapy.
