### Baroreceptor Reflex
Decreased activity of baroreceptor (sense blood pressure) afferents leads to increased sympathetic outflow and decreased parasympathetic outflow.
![[capture_20251130135805015.bmp]]
Blood vessels → α₁-adrenergic receptors → vasoconstriction
Heart (SA node, AV node, ventricles) → β₁-adrenergic receptors →↑ heart rate (positive chronotropy), ↑ contractility (positive inotropy)

Other factors increasing vascular sympathetic stimulation: 
- Chemoreceptors which detect hypoxia/hypercapnia. 
- Stimulation of skeletal muscle receptors during exercise. 
- Stimulation of visceral and cutaneous nociceptors. 
- Mental Stress.
### Cutaneous Blood Flow 
- Cutaneous vascular sympathetic nerves are not baroreceptor sensitive. 
- Cutaneous arterioles are innervated by thermosensitive sympathetic nerves. 
- Hypothermia and emotional stimuli stimulate cutaneous vasoconstriction.

## 1.4 Parasympathetic vasodilator nerves
Most vascular beds are not innervated by parasympathetic nerves. Parasympathetic nerves are not tonically active.
Particularly significant in cerebral circulation and penile erectile tissue. Main neurotransmitter is nitric oxide (NO) rather than acetylcholine (ACh).

## 3.2 Non-adrenergic Non-cholinergic (NANC) Transmission
Not all post-ganglionic autonomic transmission can be explained by NA or ACh. There is often a residual action which can be due to one or more other effectors. This is known as NANC transmission. NANC can be excitatory (ATP and Substance P) or inhibitory (neurotensin,VIP). Action of NANC is also tissue-dependent (excitatory in vas deferens, blood vessels, inhibitory in the gut). 
![[Pasted image 20251130011322.png]]
1. Classical transmitters DA (sympathetic neurones, kidney), GABA (enteric neurones), 5HT (enteric neurones). 
2. Purinergic transmission. Uses ATP as the transmitter (Post ganglionic sympathetic neurones e.g. blood vessels, vas deferens). Best characterised. Now considered to be a "classical" transmitter. Responsible for the fast excitatory post-synaptc potential and rapid phase of contraction produced by sympathetic nerve activity in many smooth muscle tissues. Subclassification of receptors into P1 and P2. These subtypes have been themselves subclassified. 
3. Peptide transmitters VIP, NPY, LHRH, prostaglandins (PG), Substance P, CGRP, ANP, histamine, NO, Endothelin. 
### Back-interaction (negative feedback) of PG (PGE<sub>2</sub>, PGI<sub>2</sub>) release in sympathetic nerve
NA release → α₁ activation → Ca²⁺ increase → activation of phospholipase A₂ → arachidonic acid → PG synthesis.
Once released, PGs bind presynaptic prostanoid receptors on the sympathetic nerve terminal<font color="#00b050">(e.g., EP₂, EP₄ or EP₃ depending on tissue)</font>. PGs reduce opening of N-type Ca²⁺ channels→ ↓ Ca²⁺ influx → ↓ vesicular NA release.
Positive feedback if PGI₂ > PGE₂ in certain vascular beds:  PGs increase cAMP in the nerve terminal → enhance NA release.
4.  Nitric oxide is a short-lived gas which nevertheless acts akin to a transmitter. NO mediates relaxation of the stomach induced by vagal (PNS) stimulation and relaxation of other muscle from intrinsic NANC nerves. In addition, the vasodilator effects of ACh, bradykinin etc. are also via NO. The mechanism of action is via an increase in cGMP.

# Example Question

## Q1. Explain how the autonomic nervous system regulates cardiovascular function, including sympathetic, parasympathetic, and hormonal control.

The autonomic nervous system (ANS) regulates cardiovascular function primarily through sympathetic and parasympathetic pathways, integrated with hormonal systems such as angiotensin II and vasopressin. Sympathetic nerves provide tonic vasoconstrictor input to most vascular beds via noradrenaline acting on α₁-adrenoceptors (Gq-coupled), causing smooth muscle contraction and increased total peripheral resistance, thereby maintaining blood pressure. In the heart, β₁-adrenoceptors (Gs-coupled) in the SA node, AV node, and myocardium increase heart rate (positive chronotropy) and contractility (positive inotropy) via increased cAMP and Ca²⁺. Parasympathetic control,<font color="#c00000"> mediated mainly by the vagus nerve</font>, reduces <font color="#c00000">heart rate </font>through acetylcholine acting on<font color="#c00000"> M₂ muscarinic receptors (Gi-coupled)</font>, decreasing cAMP and increasing K⁺ conductance. Most blood vessels lack direct parasympathetic innervation, but vasodilation in specific tissues such as cerebral and erectile tissue occurs via nitric oxide (NO), which activates soluble guanylate cyclase to increase cGMP and relax vascular smooth muscle. The baroreceptor reflex is critical: reduced blood pressure decreases baroreceptor firing,<font color="#c00000"> increasing sympathetic and reducing parasympathetic outflow to restore pressure</font>. Hormonal systems reinforce this control; angiotensin II causes <font color="#c00000">AT₁-mediated vasoconstriction</font> and <font color="#c00000">enhances sympathetic activity</font>, while vasopressin promotes <font color="#c00000">V₁-mediated vasoconstriction</font> and <font color="#c00000">V₂-mediated renal water retention</font>. Pharmacologically, β-blockers such as propranolol reduce sympathetic cardiac stimulation, lowering blood pressure, but may cause bradycardia or bronchoconstriction. Thus, cardiovascular autonomic regulation is a coordinated neurohumoral system essential for maintaining circulatory homeostasis.

## Q2. Compare sympathetic and parasympathetic neurotransmission in the peripheral nervous system, including receptor types and pharmacological targets.

Sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions of the peripheral nervous system share a common preganglionic cholinergic mechanism but differ significantly in postganglionic neurotransmission, receptor types, and physiological effects. In both systems, preganglionic neurons release acetylcholine onto nicotinic neuronal receptors (Nn), which are ligand-gated ion channels in <font color="#c00000">autonomic ganglia</font>. In the sympathetic system, most postganglionic neurons release noradrenaline onto adrenoceptors: α₁ receptors (Gq) mediate vasoconstriction, β₁ receptors (Gs) increase cardiac output, and β₂ receptors (Gs) mediate bronchodilation and skeletal muscle vasodilation. A key exception is <font color="#c00000">sweat glands</font>, where sympathetic postganglionic fibres release acetylcholine onto muscarinic receptors. In contrast, parasympathetic postganglionic neurons release acetylcholine onto muscarinic receptors, mainly <font color="#c00000">M₂ (Gi) in the heart</font> and <font color="#c00000">M₃ (Gq) in glands and smooth muscle</font>, producing effects such as <font color="#c00000">bradycardia, glandular secretion, bronchoconstriction, and increased gastrointestinal motility</font>. Pharmacological manipulation differs accordingly. <font color="#c00000">Atropine</font>, a muscarinic antagonist, inhibits parasympathetic activity and is used clinically to treat<font color="#c00000"> bradycardia </font>or <font color="#c00000">reduce secretions during surgery</font>, though it may cause <font color="#c00000">dry mouth and blurred vision</font>. <font color="#c00000">Propranolol</font>, a β-adrenoceptor antagonist, reduces <font color="#c00000">sympathetic cardiac effects</font> and is widely used for hypertension and arrhythmias, but may worsen <font color="#c00000">asthma due to β₂ blockade</font>. <font color="#c00000">Hexamethonium</font> blocks ganglionic nicotinic receptors in both systems, producing <font color="#c00000">widespread autonomic inhibition</font> but <font color="#c00000">severe non-selective side effects</font>. Therefore, while both systems use acetylcholine at ganglia, their distinct postganglionic transmitters and receptor distributions allow selective pharmacological targeting.

## Q3. Discuss ganglionic blockers as pharmacological agents, including their mechanism, therapeutic uses, and limitations.

Ganglionic blockers are drugs that inhibit neurotransmission at autonomic ganglia by antagonising nicotinic neuronal (Nn) acetylcholine receptors. These receptors are ligand-gated ion channels responsible for fast synaptic transmission between preganglionic and postganglionic neurons in both sympathetic and parasympathetic systems. Drugs such as hexamethonium block these receptors, thereby suppressing autonomic outflow globally. Because both divisions are inhibited, physiological effects depend on the dominant resting tone of the target organ. For example, blood vessels are primarily under sympathetic tone, so ganglionic blockade causes vasodilation and reduced blood pressure, while the heart, under predominant parasympathetic tone, may develop tachycardia. Additional effects include reduced glandular secretion, constipation, urinary retention, and postural hypotension. Historically, ganglionic blockers were used in severe hypertension because of their potent blood pressure-lowering effects. However, their therapeutic use is now extremely limited due to poor selectivity and widespread adverse effects. Modern drugs such as β-blockers, α₁ antagonists, and ACE inhibitors provide more targeted cardiovascular control with improved safety profiles. Ganglionic blockers remain useful mainly in experimental pharmacology for studying autonomic function. Their major limitation is non-selective autonomic suppression, leading to substantial systemic toxicity, including dry mouth, blurred vision, sexual dysfunction, and impaired gastrointestinal motility. Thus, although ganglionic blockers were historically important, their clinical value has largely been replaced by receptor-selective agents with superior therapeutic indices.
