
# 1. MDD

= Major Depressive Disorder

|Feature|Key Points|
|---|---|
|Prevalence|Lifetime prevalence ~17%; ~4 million affected in UK|
|Global burden|3rd leading cause of disability worldwide|
|Mortality|Suicide is major cause of death in young adults|
|Clinical issue|Heterogeneous disorder with multiple symptom clusters|
|Treatment challenge|High rates of treatment resistance|

## Core symptoms

### Psychological:

- Low mood
- Anhedonia
- Guilt
- Hopelessness
- Suicidal ideation

### Biological:

- Sleep/appetite disturbance
- Fatigue
- Cognitive slowing
- Libido loss

### Behavioural:

- Psychomotor retardation/agitation
- Self-neglect

# 2. Pathophysiological Causes of Depression

### A. Monoamine hypothesis

- Reduced 5-HT
- Reduced NA
- Possible DA dysfunction

### B. Circuit pathology

- Dysfunctional **medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC)**
- Altered **anterior cingulate cortex (ACC)**
- Reward circuit deficits
- Negative emotional bias circuits

### C. Neuroplasticity hypothesis

- Chronic stress → ↓ BDNF
- ↓ Synaptogenesis
- ↓ Dendritic spine density
- Impaired cortical connectivity

- Selective 5-HT / NA reuptake inhibitors

## Effect

- Immediate action: crosses BBB and reaches pharmacological targets within 4 hrs. Mininal effect after an acute response.
- Delayed onset: takes 4-6 weaks for produce clinical response.

## Mechanism

### 1. Immediate Action

#### 1. Acute Response

- Drug crosses BBB
- Blocks SERT/NET → ↑ Synaptic 5-HT/NA 

#### 2. Consequence Effects

- 5-HT activates presynaptic 5-HT₁A autoreceptors
- Immediate negative feedback: 5-HT₁A inhibits serotonergic neuron firing
- ↓ 5-HT release
- Dampen down tonings

### 2. Chronic Changes

- Continuous ↑5-HT → Desensitization of 5-HT₁A in DRN (dorsal raphe)
- Negative feedback weakens
- Serotonergic neurons increase firing
- Sustained ↑5-HT release in projection areas (PFC, hippocampus)

## Mechanism

### 1. NMDAR antagonism → Dendritic spine formaiton

 <font color="#245bdb">	Within 12 hrs</font>
- Blocks NMDAR on Sst<sup>+</sup> (somatostatin expressing) GABA interneurons
- Disinhibits glutamatergic pyramidal neurons

- → ↑ Glutamate release in ACC
  (Can be blocked by naltrexone = MOR antagonist)

# Example Questions

## 1. “Describe the major pharmacological classes of antidepressants, including mechanisms, therapeutic strengths, and limitations.”

Antidepressants can be divided into several major pharmacological classes, each targeting monoaminergic or neuroplastic mechanisms.

SSRIs, such as fluoxetine, selectively inhibit serotonin reuptake via SERT blockade, increasing synaptic 5-HT. They are first-line treatments due to safety and tolerability, though delayed onset and sexual dysfunction are major limitations.

SNRIs, such as venlafaxine, inhibit both serotonin and noradrenaline reuptake, offering broader efficacy, particularly in severe depression, but may increase blood pressure.

TCAs, including amitriptyline, inhibit monoamine reuptake but also block muscarinic, histaminergic, and adrenergic receptors, causing anticholinergic effects and cardiotoxicity despite strong efficacy.

MAOIs, such as phenelzine, inhibit monoamine breakdown and are effective in atypical depression but require <font color="#c00000">strict dietary control</font> due to <font color="#c00000">hypertensive crisis risk</font>.

<font color="#c00000">NaSSAs</font> such as <font color="#c00000">mirtazapine</font> increase NA and 5-HT release through α2 antagonism and are useful when sedation or appetite stimulation is beneficial.

Third-generation antidepressants such as ketamine represent a mechanistic shift by targeting glutamatergic circuits rather than monoamines. Ketamine produces rapid antidepressant effects by restoring synaptic plasticity.

Overall, older drugs are often effective but limited by toxicity, SSRIs/SNRIs are safer but slower and often incomplete, while newer glutamatergic therapies offer rapid efficacy with unique risks.

## 2. “Explain why SSRIs have delayed antidepressant effects despite immediate pharmacological action.”

SSRIs such as fluoxetine rapidly cross the blood-brain barrier and inhibit serotonin reuptake transporters within hours, immediately increasing synaptic serotonin concentrations. However, clinical antidepressant effects usually require 4–6 weeks.

This delay is primarily due to autoregulatory feedback mechanisms. Increased serotonin initially activates presynaptic 5-HT₁A autoreceptors in the dorsal raphe nucleus, which inhibit serotonergic neuron firing and reduce serotonin release. Thus, despite transporter blockade, acute therapeutic benefit is limited.

With chronic SSRI administration, sustained serotonin elevation gradually desensitises these inhibitory autoreceptors. As feedback suppression weakens, serotonergic neurons resume and then increase firing, producing greater serotonin release across projection regions such as the<font color="#c00000"> prefrontal cortex and hippocampus</font>.

<font color="#c00000">Over time, these neurochemical changes also promote downstream adaptations including altered gene expression, increased BDNF release, improved synaptogenesis, and restoration of dysfunctional mood-regulating circuits.</font>

Thus, delayed onset reflects the time required for receptor adaptation and neuroplastic remodelling rather than delayed drug delivery.

This limitation is clinically important because many patients remain symptomatic or suicidal during early treatment, highlighting the need for faster-acting therapies such as ketamine.

## 3. “Discuss ketamine as a third-generation antidepressant, including mechanism, strengths, and limitations.”

Ketamine represents a major advance in antidepressant pharmacology because it produces rapid and robust effects, particularly in treatment-resistant depression.

Unlike traditional monoamine-based therapies, ketamine primarily acts as an NMDA receptor antagonist. It preferentially blocks NMDA receptors on somatostatin-positive (Sst+) GABA interneurons, reducing inhibitory tone and disinhibiting glutamatergic pyramidal neurons. This produces a transient glutamate burst that stimulates AMPA signalling, increases BDNF release, enhances dendritic spine formation, and restores cortical synaptic connectivity.

These neuroplastic changes can occur within hours, producing antidepressant effects in 1–2 hours and rapidly reducing suicidal ideation.

Strengths include:
- Rapid onset
- High efficacy in treatment-resistant depression
- Novel mechanism
- Potential rescue therapy for acute suicidality

Limitations include:
- Dissociation
- Perceptual disturbance
- Anxiety
- Abuse liability
- Motor impairment
- Short duration of action (often <7 days)

Some evidence also suggests partial μ-opioid receptor involvement.
Thus, ketamine provides proof that depression can be treated through rapid circuit-level neuroplastic restoration rather than slow monoamine accumulation alone, though adverse effects currently limit widespread chronic use.
