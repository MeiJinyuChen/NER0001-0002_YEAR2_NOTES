
# 1. Concept

## 1.1 Definition

A progressive neurodegenerative disease caused by the preferential death of dopaminergic cells in substantia nigra

## 1.2 Symptoms

### **Motor features:**

- bradykinesia (slowed movements)
- tremor at rest
- muscular rigidity
- altered gait
- postural instability

### **Non-motor features:**

- neuropsychiatric features
- autonomic symptoms
- sleep disorders
- sensory dysfunction

# 2. Mechanism

## 2.1 Structure of Nigrostriatal Pathway

- Straitum: caudate + putamen
- Glubus pallidus: GPe + GPi
- STN (subthlamic nucleus)
- Substantia nigra: SNc (pars compacta) + SNr (pars reticulata)

## 2.2 Pathological hallmark

- Progressive loss of **dopaminergic (DA) neurons in SNc**
- ↓ dopamine in **striatum (nigrostriatal pathway)**

<img src="assets/Pasted%20image%2020260425052839.png" width="400" alt="Pasted image 20260425052839">

### **Functional consequence**

- DA normally **balances motor output via basal ganglia circuits**
- Loss → **imbalance between “GO” and “NO-GO” pathways**

## **2.3 Basal Ganglia Circuit**

-  Basal ganglia do NOT generate movement — they gate thalamic modulatory output to cortex

| <img src="assets/Pasted%20image%2020260425053010.png" width="275" alt="Pasted image 20260425053010"> | <img src="assets/Pasted%20image%2020260425053029.png" width="275" alt="Pasted image 20260425053029"> |
| ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |

### **Direct pathway (GO)**

- Cortex → Striatum (dMSN) → inhibits GPi/SNr → **disinhibits thalamus → ↑ desired movement**

### **Indirect pathway (NO-GO)**

- Cortex → Striatum (iMSN) → inhibits GPe → disinhibits STN → excites GPi/SNr → **inhibits thalamus → ↓ competing movement**

### **Hyperdirect pathway**

- Cortex → excites STN → excites GPi/SNr → inhibits thalamus → **rapid suppression of movement**

## **2.4 SNc Modulation to Basal Ganglia Circuit**

|       | Target          | Receptor | G-protein | Effect         | Functional Outcome                                  |
| ----- | --------------- | -------- | --------- | -------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| SNc → | dMSN (Direct)   | **D1**   | Gs        | ↑ cAMP → ↑ PKA | ↑ excitability → **↑ movement facilitation (GO)**   |
| SNc → | iMSN (Indirect) | **D2**   | Gi        | ↓ cAMP → ↓ PKA | ↓ excitability → **↓ movement suppression (NO-GO)** |
- **Net dopamine effect: promotes movement**

## **2.5 Dysfunction in Parkinson’s Disease**

- ↓ DA release fom SNc →
    - **Direct pathway underactive** (weak GO)
    - **Indirect pathway overactive** (excess NO-GO)
- GPi/SNr over-inhibits thalamus
- ↓ Thalamocortical excitation → **bradykinesia, rigidity**

## 2.6 Cause of PD

- Multifactorial: genetic + environmental + ageing-related cellular stress
- Resulting in SNc cell death

### Genetic factors

1. Rare causal mutations
	- Autosomal dominant
	  Examples: SNCA (α-synuclein), ...
	- Autosomal recessive
2. Common variants (moderate/low risk)
   Examples: GBA1, ...

### Environmental factors

1. Drug exposure
   Examples: MPTP
2. Infection
3. Traumatic brain injury
4. Neurotoxin exposure
5. CO poisoning

### Cellular factors

1. Protein aggregation (α-synuclein misfolds and aggregates to form Lewy bodies)
2. Mitochondrial dysfunction
3. Oxidative stress
4. ...

## 4.2 Early PD: L-DOPA + Adjuncts (Gold Standard)

- Oral L-DOPA + peripheral DDC inhibitor (carbidopa/benserazide)

### **Mechanism**

- DA cannot cross BBB
- L-DOPA crosses BBB → converted to DA by AADC
- Adjuncts (carbidopa/benserazide) inhibits peripheral AADC
  → ↑ L-DOPA reaching brain & ↓ peripheral side effects

### **Strengths**

- Most effective for motor symptoms
- Directly restores DA

### **Limitations**

- Cannot treat long-term symptoms (motor fluctuations and dyskinesias) in advanced PD

## 4.3 Advanced PD

- Intestinal/Subcutaneous infusion

### Symptoms & Mechanism

- Progressive loss of DA neurons → loss of DA buffering 
- Fluctuating L-DOPA level
  → Pulsatile striatal DA

#### Motor fluctuations

1. Wearing-off: predictable end-of-dose deterioration (due to short half-life of peripheral L-DOPA)
2. On-off: unpredictable sudden changes in mobility

#### Dyskinesias

1. Peak dose: involuntary movements during "ON"
   (reason: years of pulsatile DA → abnormal LTP in direct pathway → supersensitive D1 receptor)

### Therapeutic Strategy

#### Goals:

- Reduce L-DOPA flutuations → Smoother DA level

#### Strategies:

1. Release L-DOPA slowly
2. Add COMT & MAO-B inhibitors
3. Smaller & more frequent L-DOPA dose

# Example Question

## 1. “Describe the pathophysiology of Parkinson’s disease and explain the pharmacological rationale of current treatment strategies.”

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder caused primarily by the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta, resulting in reduced dopamine delivery to the striatum. This disrupts basal ganglia circuitry responsible for movement regulation.

Normally, dopamine stimulates D1 receptors on direct pathway neurons to promote movement and inhibits D2 receptor-mediated indirect pathway neurons to suppress excessive motor inhibition. Loss of dopamine weakens the direct “GO” pathway while overactivating the indirect “NO-GO” pathway, leading to excessive inhibition of thalamocortical motor output. This produces bradykinesia, rigidity, tremor, and postural instability.

Pharmacological treatment aims to restore dopaminergic signalling or rebalance circuitry. The gold standard is L-DOPA combined with peripheral DDC inhibitors such as carbidopa or benserazide. L-DOPA crosses the blood-brain barrier and is converted to dopamine centrally, while peripheral enzyme inhibition increases CNS delivery and reduces systemic adverse effects.

Adjunctive therapies include COMT inhibitors (entacapone, tolcapone) and MAO-B inhibitors (<font color="#c00000">selegiline</font>, rasagiline), which prolong dopamine action. Dopamine agonists such as <font color="#c00000">pramipexole</font> and ropinirole directly stimulate receptors and may delay L-DOPA use in younger patients.

<font color="#c00000">Antimuscarinic drugs</font> may improve tremor by reducing cholinergic overactivity, while <font color="#c00000">amantadine</font> can reduce dyskinesias.

Although L-DOPA remains the most effective treatment, long-term use causes <font color="#c00000">motor fluctuations</font> and <font color="#c00000">dyskinesias</font> due to <font color="#c00000">progressive neuronal loss</font> and <font color="#c00000">pulsatile dopamine stimulation</font>. Thus, current strategies focus on symptom control rather than disease modification.

## 2. “Discuss the pharmacology, strengths, and limitations of L-DOPA therapy in Parkinson’s disease.”

L-DOPA (levodopa) remains the most effective pharmacological therapy for Parkinson’s disease because it directly restores central dopamine synthesis. Dopamine itself cannot cross the blood-brain barrier, but L-DOPA can, where it is converted by aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase (AADC) into dopamine within surviving CNS neurons.

Peripheral metabolism by AADC and COMT normally limits L-DOPA bioavailability, with only a small proportion reaching the brain. Therefore, L-DOPA is administered with peripheral DDC inhibitors such as carbidopa or benserazide, which <font color="#c00000">increase CNS delivery</font> and <font color="#c00000">reduce peripheral side effects such as nausea, vomiting, hypotension, and arrhythmias</font>.

L-DOPA provides superior control of bradykinesia and rigidity compared with other therapies and remains the gold standard for motor symptom management. However, chronic therapy is limited by <font color="#c00000">progressive loss of dopamine buffering capacity</font>, leading to fluctuating plasma and striatal dopamine levels.

Major long-term complications include:

- Wearing-off
- On-off fluctuations
- Peak-dose dyskinesias

These arise from intermittent receptor stimulation and maladaptive plasticity within basal ganglia pathways.

Adjuncts such as COMT inhibitors and MAO-B inhibitors can prolong efficacy, while infusion therapies provide more continuous dopaminergic delivery in advanced disease.

Despite unmatched symptomatic efficacy, L-DOPA does not halt neurodegeneration and eventually becomes complicated by motor and psychiatric side effects.

Overall, L-DOPA is highly effective but pharmacologically limited by disease progression and long-term treatment complications.

## 3. “Compare dopamine agonists, antimuscarinic drugs, and advanced therapies in Parkinson’s disease.”

Beyond L-DOPA, several alternative or adjunctive therapies are used in Parkinson’s disease depending on disease stage, patient age, and symptom profile.

Dopamine agonists such as pramipexole, ropinirole, rotigotine, and apomorphine directly stimulate dopamine receptors, primarily D2-family receptors. Their major strengths include longer half-life, smoother dopaminergic stimulation, and delayed need for L-DOPA in younger patients. However, they are less effective for severe motor symptoms and commonly cause hallucinations, postural hypotension, and impulse control disorders.

Antimuscarinic drugs such as procyclidine and trihexyphenidyl reduce overactive cholinergic interneuron signalling in the striatum, partially correcting neurotransmitter imbalance. They are mainly useful for tremor-dominant PD in younger patients. Limitations include poor efficacy for bradykinesia and significant cognitive side effects, particularly in older patients.

Amantadine increases dopamine availability and antagonises NMDA receptors, offering modest symptomatic benefit and useful dyskinesia reduction.

In advanced PD, continuous intestinal or subcutaneous L-DOPA infusion can reduce pulsatile stimulation and motor fluctuations. Deep brain stimulation (DBS), typically targeting the subthalamic nucleus or globus pallidus interna, modulates abnormal basal ganglia activity and substantially improves refractory motor symptoms.

Thus, dopamine agonists are valuable early or adjunctive therapies, antimuscarinics are limited niche treatments, and advanced device-based therapies become increasingly important in later-stage disease. Treatment choice depends on balancing efficacy, tolerability, and disease progression.
