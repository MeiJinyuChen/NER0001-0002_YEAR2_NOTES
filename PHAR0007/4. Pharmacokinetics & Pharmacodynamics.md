
# 1. Concepts 

## 1.1 Definition

### Pharmacodynamics (PD)

- Study of what drug does to body
- Biochemical and physiological effects of drugs in body & Mechanism of drug action
- Includes:
    - Receptor interactions
    - Signalling mechanisms

### **Pharmacokinetics (PK)**

- Study of what body does to drug
- Time course of drug concentration in body
- Includes:
	- Metabolites
	- Mathematical interpretation of data

## 1.2 Why Pharmacokinetics Matters 

- Ideal drug regime: correct concentration at target site
- Avoid toxicity / adverse drug reactions
- Enable individualised dosing
- Predict drug interactions
- Plasma concentration ≠ tissue concentration

## 1.3 ADME Framework 

### **A – Absorption**

- Movement from administration site → bloodstream

### **D – Distribution**

- Reversible transfer from blood → tissues

### M – Metabolism 

- Chemical modification (mainly liver)

### **E – Excretion**

- Removal from body (mainly kidney)

Note:Drug elimination = metabolism + excretion

# **4. Distribution**

## 4.1 **Definition**

- Reversible transfer of drug between compartments

## 4.2 Determinants

- Blood flow (perfusion)
- Membrane permeability
- Tissue binding
- Lipid solubility

## **4.3 Distribution patterns**

1. Mainly in vascular system
2. Mainly in body water
	- Low molecular weight
	- Soluble
3. Tissue-specific accumulation
	- e.g. liver (typical), fat tissue
4. Non-uniform distribution

## **4.4 V<sub>D</sub> (Apprarent Volume of Distribution)**

### **Definition**

- Volume of fluid which drug would occupy if it were evenly distributed through that volume at the concentration measured in plasma
- V<sub>D</sub> = Dose / C<sub>p</sub><sup>0</sup>

### **Interpretation**

- Low VD → drug stays in vascular system
	- e.g. insulin
- High VD → drug accumulates in tissues
	- e.g. chloroquine

## **4.5 Plasma Protein Binding**

### **Binding proteins**

- Albumin (acidic drugs)
- α<sub>1</sub>-acid glycoprotein (basic drugs)
- Globulins

### Binding consequences

|              | Consequence                                                                                                                          | Mechanism                                                                                                           | Therapy                                                                          |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Activity     | ↓                                                                                                                                    | Cannot bind to receptors                                                                                            |                                                                                  |
| Absorption   | ↑                                                                                                                                    | Concentration gradient (sink effect)                                                                                |                                                                                  |
| Distribution | Restricted to the distribution of the protein                                                                                        |                                                                                                                     |                                                                                  |
| Storage      | Stay longer in body                                                                                                                  |                                                                                                                     |                                                                                  |
| Elimination  | ↓                                                                                                                                    | No globular filtration of bound drug                                                                                |                                                                                  |
| Displacement | <font color="#c00000">↑ Activity of the displaced drug</font> if a second drug with similar physiological characteristics is applied | Drugs with similar physiochemical (binding) characteristics can compete and displace<br>→ Release of the dirst drug | Important for highly bound drug with a narrow therapeutic index<br>e.g. warfarin |
Example of displacement:
- 98 % drug is bound and 2 % is free If only 2 % are displaced this results in 96 % drug is bound and 4 % is free an increase in free drug of 100 %

# 6. Excretion

## **6.1 Renal Excretion**

- Occurs in kidney

### **Three processes**

1. Glomerular filtration
2. Tubular secretion
3. Tubular reabsorption

## 6.2 **Glomerular Filtration**

- Passive process
	- No active transport

| Drug Type                                               | Effect                    | Concentration            |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Free drug                                               | Filtrated                 | In filtrate = In plasma  |
| Drug largely bound to plasma protein<br>(e.g. warfarin) | Not efficiently filtrated | In filtrate << In plasma |

## **6.3 Tubular Secretion**

1. Active transport
	- Limited transporter capacity
2. Drugs with similar structure compete for transporters
- → Can be saturated
	- → Zero order kinetic
	- → ↑ Risk of drug accumulation
	- → ↑ Toxity
- Drug bound to plasma protein can also be eliminated

## 6.4 **Reabsorption**

- Lipid-soluble drugs reabsorbed
- Influenced by urine pH
	- ↓ pH → ↑ Reabsorption of weak acids
	- Clinical use: adjust urine pH to decrease reabsorption for over-dose patient

# Example Question

## 1. “Describe the ADME principles of pharmacokinetics and explain their therapeutic significance.”

Pharmacokinetics describes the time course of drug handling by the body and is classically divided into absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME).

<font color="#c00000">Absorption refers to movement from the administration site into systemic circulation.</font> It depends on lipid solubility, ionisation state, membrane permeability, and route of administration. Oral drugs are particularly influenced by gut conditions and first-pass hepatic metabolism, reducing bioavailability.

<font color="#c00000">Distribution involves reversible transfer between plasma and tissues. </font>Key determinants include blood flow, plasma protein binding, tissue affinity, and blood-brain barrier permeability. Volume of distribution (Vd) helps predict whether a drug remains largely intravascular or accumulates in tissues.

Metabolism primarily occurs in the liver via cytochrome P450 enzymes. Phase I reactions functionalise drugs, while Phase II conjugation increases water solubility for elimination. Metabolism may inactivate drugs, activate prodrugs, or occasionally generate toxic metabolites.

<font color="#c00000">Excretion occurs mainly through renal filtration, secretion, and reabsorption.</font> Urine pH significantly influences elimination of weak acids and bases.

These processes collectively determine therapeutic concentration, dosing frequency, toxicity risk, and drug interactions. For example, highly protein-bound drugs such as warfarin are vulnerable to displacement interactions, while zero-order elimination drugs such as ethanol pose accumulation risks.

Overall, pharmacokinetics is essential for rational prescribing, safe dose adjustment, and individualised medicine.

## 2. “Explain how drug ionisation, lipid solubility, and route of administration influence absorption and bioavailability.”

Drug absorption and bioavailability are critically determined by membrane permeability, which depends primarily on lipid solubility and ionisation state.

Lipid-soluble, non-ionised drugs cross biological membranes more readily through passive diffusion. Drug ionisation depends on both the drug’s pKa and environmental pH, as described by the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation. Weak acids are more non-ionised in acidic environments, while weak bases are more non-ionised in alkaline environments.

Ion trapping occurs when drugs become ionised after crossing into compartments with different pH, causing accumulation.

Route of administration strongly influences bioavailability. Intravenous administration provides 100% bioavailability because it bypasses absorption barriers entirely. Oral administration is convenient but limited by incomplete absorption, gut degradation, and first-pass hepatic metabolism. Sublingual, rectal, inhaled, and parenteral routes may partially or completely bypass first-pass effects.

Bioavailability is quantitatively measured by area under the plasma concentration-time curve (AUC).

Clinically, these principles influence drug formulation, dosing, and toxicity management. For example, morphine has reduced oral bioavailability due to significant first-pass metabolism, while nitroglycerin is given sublingually to maximise rapid systemic delivery.

Thus, understanding physicochemical drug properties and administration routes is essential for optimising therapeutic efficacy.

## 3. “Discuss the pharmacological importance of drug distribution, metabolism, and elimination.”

Drug distribution, metabolism, and elimination critically determine therapeutic efficacy, duration of action, and toxicity.

Distribution depends on blood flow, membrane permeability, tissue binding, and plasma protein binding. Drugs with low volume of distribution (Vd) remain mainly within plasma, while highly lipophilic drugs may accumulate extensively in tissues such as fat or liver. Plasma protein binding restricts free drug concentration but may prolong action and create significant displacement interactions.

The blood-brain barrier selectively restricts CNS drug access to lipophilic, non-ionised molecules, influencing CNS pharmacotherapy.

Metabolism occurs mainly in the liver via Phase I and Phase II reactions. Cytochrome P450 enzymes are particularly important in oxidation and drug-drug interactions. Metabolism can terminate drug action, activate prodrugs, or generate toxic intermediates.

Enterohepatic circulation may recycle some drugs, prolonging duration and increasing toxicity risk.

Renal elimination involves glomerular filtration, tubular secretion, and tubular reabsorption. Free drug is filtered, while lipid-soluble drugs may be reabsorbed unless ionised. Urine pH manipulation can therefore enhance elimination in overdose.

Most drugs follow first-order kinetics, while a few, such as ethanol, exhibit zero-order elimination due to saturation.

Overall, understanding distribution, metabolism, and elimination is essential for safe prescribing, avoiding adverse interactions, and tailoring pharmacotherapy to patient physiology.
