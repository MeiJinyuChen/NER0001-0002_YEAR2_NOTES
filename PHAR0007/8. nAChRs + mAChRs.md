# Example Question


## 1. “Compare nicotinic and muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, including mechanisms, pharmacology, and therapeutic significance.”

Acetylcholine acts through two major receptor classes: nicotinic (nAChRs) and muscarinic (mAChRs), which differ significantly in structure, signalling, and physiological roles. nAChRs are ligand-gated ion channels located at autonomic ganglia, neuromuscular junctions (NMJ), adrenal medulla, and CNS. Activation causes rapid Na⁺ influx and membrane depolarisation, producing fast excitatory transmission. At the NMJ, this leads to skeletal muscle contraction, while in autonomic ganglia it facilitates both sympathetic and parasympathetic transmission.

mAChRs are G-protein coupled receptors located primarily on parasympathetic effector organs. M2 receptors in the heart are Gi-coupled, reducing cAMP and slowing pacemaker depolarisation, thereby decreasing heart rate. M3 receptors are Gq-coupled and stimulate IP₃/Ca²⁺ signalling, causing smooth muscle contraction and glandular secretion.

Pharmacologically, nAChRs are targeted by ganglionic blockers such as hexamethonium and NMJ blockers such as tubocurarine or suxamethonium. Competitive NMJ blockers are useful in surgery, while depolarising blockers are valuable for rapid intubation. mAChRs are targeted by agonists such as <font color="#c00000">bethanechol</font> and <font color="#c00000">pilocarpine</font> for urinary retention and glaucoma, respectively, while antagonists such as atropine and ipratropium are used in bradycardia, poisoning, and COPD.

<font color="#c00000">nAChR drugs are often limited by poor selectivity and widespread autonomic effects, whereas mAChR drugs may cause systemic anticholinergic toxicity including dry mouth, constipation, blurred vision, and confusion.</font>

Overall, understanding receptor subtype, location, and signalling is essential for predicting both therapeutic applications and adverse effects of cholinergic drugs.


## 2. “Describe the pharmacology and clinical importance of acetylcholinesterase inhibitors.”


Acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibitors increase acetylcholine concentrations at cholinergic synapses by preventing its enzymatic breakdown. This enhances signalling at both nicotinic and muscarinic receptors.

<font color="#c00000">Short-acting</font> inhibitors such as <font color="#c00000">edrophonium</font> are used diagnostically in <font color="#c00000">myasthenia gravis</font> due to their <font color="#c00000">rapid and reversible action</font>. <font color="#c00000">Medium-acting</font> agents such as <font color="#c00000">neostigmine</font> and physostigmine are therapeutically useful in <font color="#c00000">myasthenia gravis, reversal of competitive neuromuscular blockade, and occasionally in antimuscarinic poisoning</font>. Neostigmine primarily acts peripherally, while physostigmine crosses the blood-brain barrier.

Irreversible inhibitors, mainly <font color="#c00000">organophosphates</font> such as sarin and VX, covalently phosphorylate AChE, causing prolonged enzyme inactivation. This leads to dangerous accumulation of acetylcholine and continuous receptor stimulation, producing a cholinergic crisis characterised by bronchospasm, bradycardia, muscle fasciculation, paralysis, seizures, and respiratory failure.

The major therapeutic strength of reversible AChE inhibitors is improved neuromuscular transmission, particularly in diseases with impaired NMJ function. However, <font color="#c00000">adverse effects include excessive muscarinic stimulation such as salivation, diarrhoea, sweating, and bradycardia</font>.

In organophosphate poisoning, <font color="#c00000">pralidoxime</font> can reactivate AChE by cleaving the phosphate-enzyme bond before “aging” occurs. Atropine is also essential to block life-threatening muscarinic effects.

Thus, AChE inhibitors are highly important pharmacological tools with both therapeutic and toxicological significance. Their benefits depend heavily on duration of action, tissue selectivity, and careful clinical control.


## 3. “Compare muscarinic receptor agonists and antagonists in terms of mechanism, therapeutic uses, and adverse effects.”

Muscarinic receptor drugs modify parasympathetic function by either stimulating or inhibiting mAChRs located on smooth muscle, glands, heart, and eye.

Muscarinic agonists such as<font color="#c00000"> bethanechol and pilocarpine </font>directly activate mAChRs. Bethanechol primarily stimulates M3 receptors, increasing <font color="#c00000">GI and bladder smooth muscle contraction</font>, making it useful for <font color="#c00000">post-operative ileus and urinary retention</font>. Pilocarpine causes ciliary muscle contraction and increased aqueous humour drainage, lowering intraocular pressure in glaucoma, while also increasing salivary secretion in xerostomia.

The primary strength of muscarinic agonists is restoration of deficient parasympathetic function. However, <font color="#c00000">poor receptor selectivity often causes side effects such as sweating, diarrhoea, bronchospasm, hypotension, and bradycardia.</font>

Muscarinic antagonists such as atropine, ipratropium, tiotropium, oxybutynin, and tropicamide block parasympathetic activity. <font color="#c00000">Atropine</font> is widely used in <font color="#c00000">bradycardia, preoperative secretion reduction, and organophosphate poisoning</font>. <font color="#c00000">Ipratropium and tiotropium</font> treat <font color="#c00000">COPD and asthma</font> by reducing <font color="#c00000">bronchoconstriction</font>. <font color="#c00000">Oxybutynin</font> is useful in <font color="#c00000">overactive bladder</font>, while tropicamide is used for mydriasis during retinal examination.

Antimuscarinic drugs are often clinically valuable due to broad inhibition of excessive cholinergic activity, but limitations include <font color="#c00000">dry mouth, blurred vision, urinary retention, constipation, tachycardia, and cognitive impairment, especially with CNS penetration</font>.

In summary, muscarinic agonists restore parasympathetic activity whereas antagonists suppress it. Therapeutic application depends on the target organ, but adverse effects are common due to widespread receptor distribution.
