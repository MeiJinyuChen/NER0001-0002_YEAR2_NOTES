

# 1. Foundations of Spatial Vision
## Definition
Spatial vision refers to the visual system’s ability to perceive **spatial patterns of light**, forming the foundational basis for object perception.
### Core features:
- Enables perception of form, structure, edges, and detail
- Driven primarily by early visual processing stages:
    - Retina
    - Lateral Geniculate Nucleus (LGN)
    - Primary Visual Cortex (V1)

### Fourier analysis provides four critical dimensions:

| Dimension              | Function                | Psychological Significance |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Amplitude              | Determines contrast     | Light-dark difference      |
| Phase                  | Determines position     | Edge placement             |
| Spatial Frequency (SF) | Determines scale/detail | Fine vs coarse structure   |
| Orientation            | Determines angle        | Line/edge direction        |

# 2. Fourier Analysis and Spatial Representation
## Fourier Transform Principle (Fourier, 1822)
Any visual scene can be decomposed into sinusoidal wave components varying in:
- Frequency
- Amplitude
- Phase
- Orientation

### Importance:
- Complex images are represented as combinations of simpler waveforms
- Suggests visual processing may analyze scenes similarly (especially V1 simple cells)
  Evidence: V1 simple cells are orientation-selective and phase-selective

## Components Explained
### A. Amplitude
- Represents luminance contrast
- Higher amplitude = greater contrast
- Lower amplitude = reduced distinction between light and dark
### B. Phase
- Determines where luminance changes occur
- Controls spatial alignment of edges
- Measured cyclically in radians (0 to 2π)
### C. Spatial Frequency
- Measured as cycles per unit space
- Low SF:
    - Coarse
    - Large structures
    - Blurry global shape
- High SF:
    - Fine detail
    - Sharp edges
    - Texture
### D. Orientation
- Necessary for 2D image representation
- Defines edge angle:
    - Horizontal
    - Vertical
    - Oblique

# 3. Image Construction from Fourier Components
## Square Wave Construction
Sharp-edged square waves are formed by:
- Fundamental sinusoid
- Addition of odd harmonics:
    - 3F
    - 5F
    - 7F
### Rule:
Adding components of (Increasing harmonics + decreasing amplitude) = sharper image reconstruction
## Natural Scenes
Natural scenes contain:

| SF Type | Information                |
| ------- | -------------------------- |
| Low SF  | Broad shapes, coarse blobs |
| High SF | Fine details, edges        |

### Conclusion:
Object recognition requires integration of multiple spatial frequencies.

# 4. Orientation Perception
## Orientation Selectivity
The visual system contains neurons selectively responsive to specific orientations.
### Evidence: Adaptation (Gibson, 1937)
Prolonged viewing of one orientation:
- Reduces sensitivity to that orientation
- Minimal effect on dissimilar orientations
### Implications:
- Supports existence of orientation detectors
- Likely mediated by V1 neurons (Hubel & Wiesel, 1968 - find orientation-selective "V1 simple cells")

## Tilt After-effect
![[Pasted image 20260512161905.png|450]]
### Phenomenon:
After adapting to one orientation:
- Subsequent similar orientations appear repelled away from adaptor orientation
### Example:
- Vertical adaptor → slightly tilted lines appear more tilted away
### Mechanism:
- Shift in population response peak
- Suggests orientation is encoded through **population coding** rather than single neurons (<font color="#c00000">Blakemore </font>et al. 1971)
### Two Key Principles of Orientation Processing
#### 1. Adaptation
- Reduces response to constant stimuli (long-time gazing to a rod of a certain orientation)
- Enhances sensitivity to novel input
- Purpose:
  Maintains coding efficiency
#### 2. Population Coding
- Orientation is inferred from distributed neural activity
- Perception determined by peak response across neural populations

## Size after-effect
- Similar to tilt after-effect
- Due to the adaptation of cells of different sizes of RF

# 5. Spatial Frequency Processing

## Units of Measurement
### Theoretical:
- Cycles/image
### Perceptual:
- Cycles/degree (c/deg)
### Visual angle formula:
α where tan(α) = Height / Distance
### Importance:
Accounts for retinal image size rather than physical object size alone.

# 6. Contrast Sensitivity Function (CSF)
## Campbell & Robson (1968)
Measured sensitivity across SFs.
### Major findings:
- Peak sensitivity ≈ **4 cycles/degree**
- Poor sensitivity to:
    - Very low SFs
    - Very high SFs
### High SF limit:
- Defines visual acuity cutoff
- Represents maximum resolvable detail

| Spatial Frequency | Visibility |
| ----------------- | ---------- |
| Very low          | Reduced    |
| Medium (~4 c/deg) | Highest    |
| Very high         | Reduced    |

### Result:
Human vision has a specific “window of visibility.”

# 7. Developmental Changes in Spatial Vision

## Infant Vision
Compared to adults, infants have:
- Lower acuity
- Reduced sensitivity across all SFs
- Particularly poor high SF detection
- Also weaker low SF processing
### Development:
- 1 month: very poor vision
- 3 months: improving
- Adult: mature CSF
### Conclusion:
Spatial vision develops substantially postnatally.

---

# 8. Spatial Frequency Channels
## Multiple Channel Theory (Campbell & Robson, 1968)
Visual system contains multiple SF-selective channels.
### Each channel:
- Sensitive to restricted SF range
- Has different peak sensitivity
## Adaptation Evidence (Blakemore & Campbell, 1969)
Adapting to one SF:
- Reduces sensitivity primarily at that SF
- Lesser effects on adjacent SFs
### Conclusion:
Supports SF-specific detectors rather than a single broad mechanism.

---

# 9. Limits of Independent SF Access
## Hybrid Images (Schyns & Oliva, 1999)
Combining:
- High SF image
- Low SF image
### Findings:
- High SF dominates object recognition
- Low SF becomes visible when:
    - Squinting
    - Defocusing
    - Increasing distance
### Implication:
SF channels are separable but integrated into fixed perceptual outputs.

# 10. Contextual Effects in Spatial Vision
## Surround Suppression (Chubb et al., 1989)
### Phenomenon:
The perceived strength of a stimulus is reduced by a similar surrounding stimulus
- e.g. Central stimulus appears lower contrast when surrounded by same orientation
### Mechanism:
- Spatial inhibitory interactions
- Population coding across neighboring neurons

## Simultaneous Tilt Contrast
### Phenomenon:
Surrounding orientation alters perceived central orientation (让角度差距显得更夸张)
### Effect:
- Adjacent orientations appear repelled from each other
### Mechanism: Population Tilt Contrast (Blakermore et al., 1970)
- Orientation-population activity shifts because of surround interactions: neurons compete across space to determine the dominant orientation, enhancing response to novel stimuli 
- Similar to tilt after-effect

## Spatial Frequency Context Effects
- Surround influences perceived SF
- Similar principles underlie:
    - Size contrast illusions
    - Ebbinghaus/Titchener illusion
- Likely similar mechanisms of population coding

# 11. Foveal vs Peripheral Vision

## Biological Basis
### Fovea:
- High cone density
- High cortical magnification
- Superior detail resolution
### Periphery:
- Reduced cone density
- Lower cortical representation
- Worse acuity

## Peripheral Acuity
### Characteristics:
- We do not have very small RF in periphery
  → High spatial frequency & low contrast are harder to see in periphery
- Letter/object recognition worsens with eccentricity
- Larger stimuli improve performance (scaling)
### Important:
Peripheral vision is not merely blurred foveal vision.

---

# 12. Crowding: Fundamental Peripheral Limitation
## Definition
Difficulty recognizing objects in clutter despite detectability.
### Key features:
- Strong in peripheral vision; little-to-no effect in foveal vision
- Caused by nearby flankers
## Bouma’s Principle
Interference zone around target increases with eccentricity (distance to central vision).
### Consequences:
- Peripheral vision captures general gist
- Fine details lost
- Features pooled/averaged together
## Mechanistic Interpretation
Crowding errors reflect averaging of:
- Orientation
- Colour
- Shape
### Functional role:
Provides efficient summary of peripheral scenes at expense of precise identification.
