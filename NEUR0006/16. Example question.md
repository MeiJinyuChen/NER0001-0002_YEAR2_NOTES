
1. Blue-white assay

You are using the a bacterium and plasmid that allows blue-white selection to identify recombinant clones. Why is it important that the growth media used in the agar plates does NOT contain glucose?

The transcription of the truncated fragment of the E. coli lacZ, termed lacZ’, which encodes the α-peptide of the ß-galactosidase is under control of the lac operon and essential for the blue-white selection. Crucial for the blue-white selection is the hydrolysis of X-Gal by ß-galactosidase into ß-D-galactosidase and 5-bromo-4-chloro-3-hydroxyinol, which dimerizes in the presence of oxygen and results in a blue precipitate.
In the presence of glucose, the preferred energy source in many bacteria, the synthesis of cyclic-AMP (cAMP) is inhibited and the transport of cAMP out the bacterium stimulated. The low concentration of cAMP reduces the CRP/CAP (cyclic AMP receptor/catabolite gene activator protein) binding to regulatory elements in the lac operon. Without the CRP/CAP bound the RNA polymerase fails to bind efficiently to the lac operon and lacZ’ is not transcribed, resulting in all colonies being white.

2. Sounthern blot

You have prepared a Southern blot and hybridized the DNA on the blot to a single-stranded gene probe. The developed blot is blank. On reviewing your lab notes about the procedure you realise that you did not incubate the agarose gel in denaturation solution (1.5M NaCl, 0.5M NaOH) before transfer. Given this error, explain why the blot is blank.

A positive result relies upon the ability of a single stranded gene probe to hybridise to complimentary sequences on immobilised single stranded molecules of DNA. The denaturation step is required to dissociate the double-stranded DNA in order to form single stranded DNA, before transfer to the membrane to which it will be immobilized, in a state that allows binding the gene probe. If the denaturation step is forgotten it means that the DNA on the Southern blot was still in duplex form and is unable to hybridize (bind the probe). In the absence of hybridisation the added gene probe will be washed of in the steps that remove unhybridised DNA.

3. Pyrosequencing

Why is a 5'-(α-thio)-triphosphate nucleotide used for pyrosequencing.

The extension of a DNA strand with each deoxynucleotide triphosphate releases one pyrophosphate (PPi). In pyrosequencing this PPi is quantitatively converted in ATP by Sulfurylase and APS (adenosine 5’-phosphosulfate) as a substrate. A bioluminescence reaction caused by luciferin consumes this ATP and converts luciferin to oxyluciferin, in the presence of oxygen. The amount of light produced and detected by a CCD camera is proportional to the number of nucleotides incorporated. dATP needs to be replaced by (dATPαS) because dATP but not dATPαS can serve as substrate for luciferase resulting in erroneous light signals not linked to the incorporation of a deoxyadenosine triphosphate.

6. Targeting vector

When generating a conventional knock-out mouse, not using CRISPR-Cas or TALEN based strategies a thymidine kinase gene is present in the targeting vector. Explain the reason for this?

The target vector must contain regions the allow homologous recombination, a resistance gene (neomycin, positive selection marker) inserted into an exon (best to destroy also reading frame) and a thymidine kinase (negative selection marker) at the end.

The embryonic stem (ES) cells are transfected via electroporation with the linearized targeting vector.

Upon homologous recombination the thymidine kinase region is not integrated into the ES cell genome. This will generate cells resistant to neomycin that also can grow in the presence of ganciclovir, whereas random integration of the targeting vector will generate ES cells that resistant to neomycin but die in the presence of ganciclovir, because TK kinase posphorylates ganciclovir to form ganciclovir triphospate which causes chain termination and single-strand breaks upon incorporation into DNA.

This so called positive-negative selection allows to identify and enrich ES that underwent homologous recombination.

7. Plus/Minus sequencing method - Sanger

One of the first DNA sequencing methods used to obtain longer DNA sequences invented by F. Sanger (1975) is known as the “Plus and Minus” approach. Below you see a sketch of a stretch of single-stranded DNA with an annealed primer.

<img src="assets/Pasted%20image%2020260113155403.png" alt="Pasted image 20260113155403">

Sequencing by the “Plus and Minus” approach requires multiple reactions to be set up and the multiple reaction products of each of the reactions gives then the sequence.

7a.    Write down **all** reactions product for all reactions.

7b.    Sketch autoradiography bands obtained from the separation of the reaction products from the above fragments and explain how these lead to the sequence shown.

The +/- method is a DNA synthesis methods performed in two steps. In step 1 DNA is synthetized using all 4 nucleotides, one of which is radioactively labelled and DNA polymerase. This aim of reaction is to produce a random mixture of complementary DNA of various length. In step 2 these synthesis products are divided into the "plus" group and the "minus" group. Each group is then further divided into 4 reactions one for each nucleotide.

Each "plus" group reaction will be complemented with only one nucleotide (dATP, dCTP, dGTP or dTTP) and T4 polymerase. As a consequence, the T4 polymerase with use its 3' to 5' exonuclease activity but will stop at the nucleotide that is present in the plus mix.

Each "minus" group reaction will then be complemented with three nucleotides "leaving out one" and DNA polymerase I. Therefore, the minus reactions will continue to synthetize DNA until the base missing in the mixture is reached.

The reaction products of all 8 reactions are then separated on a poly-acrylamid gel and the DNA sequence can be read.
