
## Property

Linear ds DNA, 48514 bp.
Linear in phage head, but circular in cells.

-----

## Terminology

- Virulent: A phage that can only go down go down in lytic route (often due to mutants that affect the temperate ability)
- Temperate: A phage that can choose to go down in either lytic or lysogenic route
- Lysogen: A bacteria cell with phage DNA integrated into it
- Prophage: A phage DNA that is integrated into the bacterial chromosome inside a lysogen. It is not a phage but has the potential to be a phage again
- Immunity: Lysogen is immune to the infection by another particle of the same species of phage (but not to different species)
- Induction: The process of breaking the prophage out of the bacterial chromosome, destroying the lysogenic state and sending the cell back into a lytic cycle

----

## cos end

Two 12 nucleotide 5' overhangs produced by the cut of the terminase. 
- The lambda DNA is linear in phage head, but circular in cells. The cos ends anneals together to from a double stranded circular plasmid.
- Terminase is encoded by λ litself and acts like a restriction enzyme for cos site during the replication of the  λ-DNA.  The long 5' overhangs cos ends are much more sticky than the cohesive ends generated by most of the restriction enzymes.

## Rolling circle replication

### The replication of circular phage genome requires
1. Bacterial polymerase III
2. Bacteriophage proteins O and P -- Locate the bacterial machinery (polymerase and other associated enzymes) at the oriT site
![[771aef42d1e7353d61d919b4d37ca9c5.jpg|300]]
### After replication
- When the head proteins have been made on the mega transcript produced in the late phase, there are 4 hedge proteins that come together and make a roughly spherical structure.
- 2 proteins (gpA and gpNu1) forms a terminase complex which translocates the DNA into the head structure. It binds to and recognizes a cos sequence on the linear DNA, pushing it to the phage head, scanning along the sequence until it finds another cos sequence, and then it cuts the cos sequences to make the 12bp overhanging ends. Once being cut, the cos sequences stick out of the bottom of the head and interact with the tail to form the full phage particle.
### Comparison between θ replication & rolling circle replication
- θ replication: Two strands simply melt and do not get nicked at the start. Making circular copies from a circular template. Bidirectional.
- Rolling  circle replication: Two strands are nicked at origin to get an opening up before the replication begins. Making linear copies from a circular template. Convenient for packaging the linear copies into the new phage head before lysis. Unidirectional.

## Lytic cycle

### Process
1. Phages attaches to bacterium (e.g.Tail fibre of phage get into contact with bacterium).
2. DNA injected into bacterium (tail particle insert DNA into the cell/unwrapped the cell from the protein and get into the cell wall).
3. Enzymes for DNA synthesis are made. Replication begins. Genomes, heads and tails are made.
4. DNA packaged into heads. Tails attached.
5. Cell bursts to release phage.

## Lysis or lysogeny

### Conditions where lysogeny is preferred
Lysis produces more phages and infects more cells. It is usually more preferred.
There are certain conditions that lysogeny is more preferred.
1. The bacterial cells are growing very slowly. Their machinery for protein synthesis (replication, transcription and translation) is not working well. It will go into a state of dormancy (braking down proteins and metabolism becomes 0) if the nutrient level further falls. The phage inside the cell would be lost, as its DNA would get broken down as well. The phage choose lysogeny until the condition of the cell improves.
2.  The cell is growing very well. High multiplicity of infection causes too much phage in the colony. No more host cells for newly released phages. The phage goes to lysogeny to prevent too high ratio of phages to cells.

### Can be determined by plaque appearance
Introduce phages to a bacteria lawn. Holes = plaques are formed when the host bacterium lyse. Each plaque is caused by a singe phage particle.
1. If the plaque is **clear** that you can see through it, then it is from a virulent phage.
2. If the plaque is **turbid**, it is from a temperate phage: in the centre of the plaque, there are very few host cells in lysogenic state left, which continue to grow after the lytic burst.

### Lysogenic establishment
![[capture_20251229034521872.bmp]]
- cII: cII binds to the -35 region, recruiting the host RNA polymerase. The host RNA polymerase can then interact with it and recognise the -10 region, starting the transcription of P<sub>RE</sub>. P<sub>RE</sub> itself is a weak promoter that cannot  be recognised by the host RNA polymerase.

In the presence of cII, genes controlled by following promoters can be transcribed by the host RNA polymerase -- lysogeny = prevent from lysis:
- P<sub>RE</sub>: promoter for repressor establishment
  1. Initiates cI expression
  2. Represses cro expression by antisense hybridisation
- P<sub>I</sub>: Increases expression level of integrase
  Integrase: Integrate the phage DNA into the bacterial chromosome
- P<sub>anti Q</sub>: Represses Q expression by antisense hybridisation
  Q: Product of a delayed early gene. The second anti-terminator that leads to the expression of the late genes that produce head and tail proteins

#### cI expression
1. cII recruits host RNA polymerase to P<sub>RE</sub>. P<sub>RE</sub> turns on first.
2. cII represses the expression of cro by antisense hybridisation: The transcription goes in opposite direction to that for P<sub>R</sub>. The strand transcribed also contains an mRNA sequence that is complementary to cro mRNA. The cro mRNA binds to it, forming a dsRNA that cannot be translated
3. P<sub>RE</sub> turns on cI expression.
4. cI binds to O<sub>R</sub> and O<sub>L</sub> .
5. O<sub>R</sub> turns off P<sub>R</sub> and turns on P<sub>RM</sub>, O<sub>L</sub> turns off P<sub>L</sub>.
   P<sub>RE</sub> would also be turned off if cI binding to O<sub>R</sub><sup>3</sup> is too strong.
   -- Establishment of repressor turns off further early gene expression. It prevents the lytic cycle from proceeding.

#### The operator region
- Each of the operator is made up of 3 binding sites, and each of the binding site contains 2 half sites. Each N-terminal domain from a repressor binds to one of the half sites.
- Both cI and cro can binds to operators, but to different regions with different affinity, and inhibits the binding of RNA polymerase to certain promoter sites by overlapping the promoter sequences.
**cro** 
1. cro has the highest affinity for O<sub>R</sub><sup>3</sup>. With cro binding to O<sub>R</sub><sup>3</sup>, the host RNA polymerase cannot recognise and express from P<sub>RM</sub>.
2. The Q protein is sufficient for late expression and early expression genes are not required, so the lysis pathway is already determined. In this situration, it also binds to O<sub>R</sub><sup>1</sup>, O<sub>L</sub><sup>1</sup> at high concentration to turn off P<sub>L</sub> and P<sub>R</sub>.

**cI**
cI binds most strongly to O<sub>R</sub><sup>1</sup> and O<sub>L</sub><sup>1</sup>.
1. It overlaps the RNA polymerase binding site from P<sub>L</sub> and P<sub>R</sub>. P<sub>L</sub> and P<sub>R</sub> are turned off. The expression of early genes such as virus head and tail proteins are turned off.
2. Once bound to O<sub>R</sub><sup>1</sup>/O<sub>L</sub><sup>1</sup>, cI gets cooperative binding sites to O<sub>R</sub><sup>2</sup>/O<sub>L</sub><sup>2</sup>. Host RNA polymerase is able to recognise the weak binding sites for P<sub>RM</sub> because of its interaction with cI at O<sub>R</sub><sup>2</sup>.
3. If the concentration of cI is too high (the induction stage can never happen, so it is not beneficial to the phage): cI will also binds to O<sub>R</sub><sup>3</sup> and turn off P<sub>RM</sub>.
   The concentration of cI in bacteria is auto-regulated.

### Lysogenic maintenance
1. cI binds to O<sub>R</sub> and O<sub>L</sub>.
2. cI is continuously transcribed from P<sub>RM</sub>.
#### cI (repressor of lysis)
##### Long-term expression
  - cI cannot have long-term expression from P<sub>RE</sub> because the turning on of P<sub>RE</sub> requires cII &cIII, which is expressed from P<sub>R</sub>, that will be turned off by cI. Is continuously made from P<sub>RM</sub> once entering the lysogeny.
- P<sub>RM</sub>: promoter for repressor maintenance.
##### Structure
![[capture_20251229064328401.bmp|300]]

The 2 terminal domains of cI monomer have different functions:
1. N-terminal domain: associated with DNA operator binding characteristics. 
2. C-terminal domain: responsible for the dimerization. 
   2 C-terminal domains in a dimmer interacts together to hold the lambda repressor in it dimeric form. It further orientate the 2 N-terminal domains in a way that they fit into DNA and tightly bind to the operator sequences (N-terminal domain can still bind to DNA without dimerization, but with a much lower efficiency).
   Dimerized structure is essential for efficient binding to DNA.

In induction stage:
Linker (40 amino acids) is cleaved. It reduces the affinity for the operator and induces the lysogen back to a lytic cycle.
Specific conditions. E.g. Exposing to UV light generates a specific protease that cleaves this region and causes the loss of the dimeric form.

#### Immunity

![[Pasted image 20251229144101.png|375]]

Immunity in lysogeny:
The repressor immediately expresses and presents all the time during lysogeny once the DNA has been injected into the cell. It binds to the lambda DNA in excess. So the induction transcription cycle can never start.
Why it can’t lead to immunity to other type of phages: The lambda repressor binds specifically to the operator associated to its own promoters.

### Lambda DNA integration
Circular lambda DNA is integrated into the bacterial chromosome in the final stage of establishing lysogeny.

#### Lambda insertion
![[capture_20251229080422913.bmp]]
##### Integration
**Intasome complex**
- **Int (integrase)**
  An enzyme that does the cutting and crossing over of the DNA.
  Transcribed from a delayed early gene.
- **IHF (integration host factor)**
  A bacterial protein used in the integration process.
  Bends DNA to intasome to favour the happening of integration.
**Process**
1.  Cleavage of the DNA at O region
2. Joining of one end of the phage DNA (P region) to the other end of the bacterial chromosomal DNA (B’ region)
###### site-specific recombination
Phage DNA integrates into the chromosomal DNA by recombination between the attP site on the phage DNA and attB on the bacterial DNA.
The process all happens in the intasome complex.
Integrase cannot recognise att sites by its own. It need IHF to help bend DNA to the intasome to allow the interaction.
![[capture_20251229111748867.bmp]]

##### Excision
**Excisome**
- **Xis**
  Binds to and recognises attL and attR, initiating the reverse crossing over event. 
  Bends DNA differently, alters Int binding pattern to favour excision for the intasome complex.
- **Intasome complex (Int and IHF)**
**Process**
1. Reverse crossing over event happening. The phage DNA is released.
2. Cells go back to lytic cycle after induction.

## Lytic cycle initiation
- When cro binds to O<sub>R</sub><sup>3</sup> , it prevents the RNA polymerase from binding to P<sub>RM</sub>, thus blocking the maintenance of repressor protein. 
- When cro binds to other operator sites, it prevents RNA polymerase from binding to P<sub>L</sub> and P<sub>R</sub>, turning off the early gene expression. It prevents the bacteria from going back into lysogeny.
