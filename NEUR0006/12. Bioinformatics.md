## Databank and database
### Databank 
- A collection of data (normally in simple text files) without a fixed associated query tool 
- You can download all the raw data and handle it however you wish 
#### Primary databanks
- Raw sequence/structure data
- Possibly with detailed annotations 

1. **Genbank, EMBL-ENA, DDBJ, UniProtKB/Swissprot (PIR)** 
- Simply contain sequence data (DNA or protein)
- May also have ‘feature’ information (splice sites, signal sequences, disulphides, active sites, etc.)
- DNA databanks may also contain translations (known or predicted)

IMPORTANT: Many sequences are predicted from genomic data, and gene-prediction methods are imperfect. Also, information about translational products often comes from prediction. A protein identified from genome data is hypothetical until verified by experiment.

2. **PDB (protein databank)**
- Structural data 

3. **Enzyme databank**
- Enzyme classifications (EC (enzyme commission) numbers)

##### Heuristics
A very fast approximate method.
Index the database by finding locations of short ‘words’– Take ‘words’ from the probe sequence and look them up in the index– Look for multiple matches and extend to find likely hits to full alignment

#### Secondary 
- Derived data - sequence profiles, etc 
- Generally highly annotated
### Database 
- A structured collection of data hidden behind a fixed search tool

## Homology
Homologous proteins/genes have descended from a common ancestor.
Homologues have similar structures & functions.
- Yes or no. It is wrong to say "...percentage homology"

### Homologs
1. Orthologues: Derived from different species (e.g. haemoglobin in humans and rats)
2. Paralogs: Derived from gene duplication within the same species (e.g. haemoglobin & myoglobin in humans)
### Sequence identity
Percentage sequence identity (or similarity) can be used to infer homology.
- Sequences of 100 aa with >35% identity are always homologues 
- Sequences with much lower sequence identity may be homologous

## Alignment

|           | Global alignment                                                                                              | Local alignment                                                                                                                        |
| --------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Algorithm | Needleman & Wunsch                                                                                            | Smith & Waterman                                                                                                                       |
| Method    | Aligns two sequences end-to-end, maximizing overall similarity across their entire length by adding gaps.     | Identifies regions of highest similarity​ within sequences, ignoring dissimilar parts.                                                 |
| Best use  | Comparing highly homologous​ sequences of similar length (e.g., orthologous proteins from different species). | Finding conserved domains or motifs​ in otherwise divergent sequences (e.g., shared functional domains in different protein families). |
| Flaw      | Introduces many gaps in less similar regions.                                                                 | Ignore allotopic similar regions.                                                                                                      |

## Identifying homologs

![[Pasted image 20260109133151.png|525]]
- High Identity (40%-100%): Use Automatic Pairwise Alignment​ (e.g., BLAST, FASTA).
- Medium Identity (20%-40%): Use consensus sequence.
- Twilight zone (0~20%): Random sequence can show similarity of up to 20%
  Low identity (10%~20%): Use profile​ method. 
  Very Low Identity (<20%): Rely on Structure Prediction, as 3D structure is more conserved than sequence.
